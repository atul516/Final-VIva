# Viva Defence Support

**Thesis:** *Corporate Responsibility Regarding the Concern of Greenwashing, Policy and Legal Initiatives — A Critical Study*

Use this as a speaking crib, not an essay. Each cluster is one line of questioning. Read the **Answer** aloud. Use **If pressed** only if the examiner follows up.

The rule for every answer: concede the fair point, restate the narrower claim you can defend, then return to the thesis.

---

## 0. Thirty-second core

Greenwashing in India is no longer only a false advertisement. It is a misleading environmental claim that can appear on a label, in a campaign, in a BRSR filing, or in a green-debt document. India already has the legal hooks — Section 166(2), SEBI’s BRSR, and the Consumer Protection Act — but they do not work as one system. Duties exist; verification, standing, coordination, and deterrent penalties do not. The original contribution is not the discovery of greenwashing. It is the integrated mapping of one claim across company law, securities law, and consumer law, and a sequenced three-statute design to close that gap.

## 1. Opening: problem, contribution, and “why a law thesis”

**Clubbed questions**

- State the research problem in one sentence. Why is it legal, not only ethical?
- What is your original contribution? What did you add after Varottil, CCLI, Bowen, Vollero, OECD, CCPA 2024, and SEBI BRSR?
- Why is this a PhD in law and not a policy report?
- If we remember only one finding, what should it be?
- What would falsify the thesis?
- What is the difference between this work and a Law Commission report?

**Answer**

The legal problem is this: Indian law can describe a misleading environmental claim, but it cannot reliably convert that description into board-level liability.

The original contribution is integration. Prior work identified pieces — a weak Section 166(2), a verification gap, fragmentation, and consumer-facing guidelines. I treat one environmental claim as a lifecycle event and ask, at each stage, who owes the duty, who holds the correlative right, who verifies, and who can punish. That Hohfeldian and three-pillar mapping, plus the finding that the enforcement gap is a design problem and not only an implementation problem, is the doctrinal work.

This is a law thesis because the claims are about *lex lata* and *lex ferenda*: what Section 166(2), BRSR, PFUTP, and the CPA already do, where they fail as legal relations, and what statutory amendments would change those relations. A policy report can recommend a council. A legal thesis has to show why a duty without a claimant is not an enforceable duty.

If only one finding is kept: India does not lack anti-greenwashing principles. It lacks the connecting design — verification before publication, a usable enforcement trigger, regulator-to-regulator referral, and a penalty that is not cheaper than the marketing budget.

What would falsify it: routine, named, financial enforcement by SEBI, CCPA, or MCA against misleading environmental claims, with director-level consequences and cross-referral, would weaken H2 and H3. It would not automatically defeat H1, because governance can still be the right *location* of the duty even if enforcement improves.

**If pressed — “isn’t this already known?”**

Yes, the *diagnosis* of a dead-letter 166(2) is known. The contribution is the *account of why* consumer, securities, and company-law tools fail at different points of the same claim, and a reform sequence that follows from that account.

## 2. Definition, evolution, and the lifecycle claim

**Clubbed questions**

- What is your operative definition, and why not TerraChoice, Vollero, or ESMA?
- The CCPA Guidelines already define greenwashing. Why is that insufficient?
- Is the “lifecycle” one legal wrong, or several wrongs sharing a slogan?
- Where is the line between puffery, net-zero aspiration, futurewashing, and a false statement of fact?
- If every long-horizon pledge needs a funded plan, have you criminalised ordinary corporate strategy?

**Answer**

I use a functional definition: greenwashing is any misleading, exaggerated, vague, or unsubstantiated environmental claim by an entity. I do not need a new academic definition. I need a definition that can travel across product claims, corporate narratives, and investor disclosures.

I prefer it to TerraChoice’s seven sins because those are marketing typologies, not liability tests. Vollero shows definitional fragmentation; that is a literature finding, not a legal test. ESMA’s definition is finance-specific. The CCPA definition is the right *consumer-law* definition — concealment, unsubstantiated claims, and misleading imagery — but it lives in guidelines, is advertisement-oriented, and does not reach BRSR, green debt, or board approval.

The lifecycle is not one statutory offence. It is one *course of conduct* that Indian law currently slices into different wrongs depending on where the sentence appears. A “carbon-neutral” claim on a pack, in an ad, in a BRSR, and in a green-bond document is commercially the same representation. Legally it can be an unfair trade practice, a disclosure issue, or nobody’s problem. That slicing is the problem.

The line is evidence, not vocabulary.

- Puffery: non-measurable sales talk with no reasonable reliance (“the greenest spirit”).
- Aspirational strategy: a future goal *with* a baseline, interim targets, budget, and a plan.
- Futurewashing: a distant date used as a substitute for present proof.
- False statement of fact: a present-tense claim that is untrue or unsubstantiated (“100% recycled,” “carbon neutral,” “net-zero aligned”).

I am not criminalising strategy. I am saying that a date-stamped environmental claim that the market will treat as a fact must carry a plan. A confidential board climate strategy with no public claim is not greenwashing.

**If pressed — “whose definition governs today?”**

For advertisements, CCPA plus CPA Sections 2(28) and 2(47). For listed-company filings, SEBI disclosure and PFUTP. For directors, Section 166(2) as an unused overlay. The gap is that those definitions are not harmonised.

## 3. The conceptual kill-shot: lying versus harming the environment

**Clubbed questions**

- Is greenwashing a failure to protect the environment, or a failure of truthful communication?
- A firm can pollute honestly, or greenwash while greening. How does a false claim become a breach of Section 166(2)?
- Polluter Pays and Public Trust deal with discharges. Which environmental doctrine actually decides a deception case?

**Answer**

Greenwashing is first a truth-and-governance wrong. It is not the same thing as pollution.

A company can pollute and disclose honestly. That is an environmental-law case, not a greenwashing case. A company can also cut emissions and still lie about being “carbon neutral.” That is greenwashing.

The link to Section 166(2) is instrumental, and I must not overstate it. The duty is to act in good faith for the protection of the environment. A board that authorises a false environmental claim does two legally relevant things. First, it acts otherwise than in good faith in a representation that the statute has made environmentally salient. Second, it frustrates environmental protection in fact: it diverts green capital, delays pressure to decarbonise, and treats a slogan as compliance. That is how a communication wrong becomes a breach of the environmental limb. It is not because every lie emits carbon.

Polluter Pays and Public Trust are not the ratio for a greenwashing case. They supply the constitutional and environmental background. The operative doctrines are misleading-claim law, disclosure law, and fiduciary good faith.

**If pressed — “so 166(2) does not mention claims at all”**

Correct. I am not saying the text names greenwashing. I am saying a purposive reading of “good faith” plus “protection of environment” can reach authorised misrepresentation. That reading is untested. That is a finding, not a weakness I hide.

## 4. Why consumer law is necessary but not sufficient

**Clubbed questions**

- CPA 2019 and the 2024 Guidelines already exist. Why are they structurally insufficient, rather than merely young?
- Which of the seven limitations is decisive?
- Is “reactive” a defect of all private law?
- Class actions and reversed burden of proof could fix asymmetry without rewriting the Companies Act. Why not that route?
- Are the Guidelines already binding?
- ASCI modified 211 ads. Is that not the system working?

**Answer**

Consumer law is necessary. It is the right tool for a false label or a false TV ad. It is not sufficient for contemporary greenwashing, because the unit of deception has moved into board-approved reports, transition pledges, and green finance.

The decisive limitation is not “reactive.” It is this: consumer law regulates the *message after it has entered the market*. It does not create a board duty to verify the claim, does not give the consumer emissions data, and does not attach personal consequences to the people who approved the claim. Fixing burden of proof inside CPA would help advertisements. It would still leave BRSR, net-zero roadmaps, and green bonds untouched.

On legal force: the Guidelines are not a dead letter. They operationalise existing CPA categories of misleading advertisement and unfair trade practice, and Section 21 already supplies penalties. I do not deny that. I say two narrower things. First, they do not independently create offences or calibrated greenwashing penalties. Second, guidelines lack the parliamentary laying and stability of rules under Section 101. Convert them into Rules, and the consumer pillar becomes stronger. It still remains a consumer pillar.

ASCI’s 211 modifications show that *self-regulation of advertising* can catch bad copy. They do not show director liability, securities enforcement, or board-level prevention. A correction of an ad is not an accountability system for ESG claims.

If I could fix only one consumer-law defect, I would elevate the Guidelines to Rules and narrow puffery for measurable environmental claims. I would still need the governance pathway for everything that is not an advertisement.

**If pressed — “so you want both?”**

Yes. Consumer law for the market-facing claim; company and securities law for the people and systems that produced it. The thesis is against a consumer-*only* framing, not against consumer law.

## 5. Methodology and “testing” hypotheses

**Clubbed questions**

- How does a doctrinal study test a hypothesis?
- H1 says “primarily.” That is causal. What alternatives did you consider?
- Strong-governance firms still greenwash. Does that kill H1?
- Is H2 tautological because you defined deterrence as turnover-linked fines?
- Overlap of regulators can mean more enforcement. Why is it a weakness?
- No interviews, no BRSR coding, no court-file study. Why accept “enforcement design”?
- Why Hohfeld?
- Name two authors you disagree with.
- What would you change if you rewrote the thesis today?

**Answer**

I did not run a statistical test. In a doctrinal thesis, a hypothesis is tested against three legal questions: the scope of the wrong, the allocation of duty, and the enforcement pathway. If those materials cannot generate real liability, the hypothesis is supported. That is not empirics, and I do not claim it is.

H1’s word “primarily” is about the *proper legal location* of the problem, not a claim that governance quality is the only social cause. Market incentives, missing LCA data, supply-chain opacity, and consumer credulity all exist. I do not deny them. I say they operate *through* boards, disclosure systems, and assurance choices. That is why the legal response belongs in governance and securities law, not only in advertising law.

Strong-governance jurisdictions still see greenwashing. That does not kill H1. It shows that governance duties fail unless they are paired with verification and deterrence — which is H2 and H3. Volkswagen is an argument for my combined hypotheses, not against H1.

H2 is not “India has no penalties, therefore India has no penalties.” Duties, Section 21, Section 166(7), and PFUTP exist. H2 is that the *routine* pathway for an ordinary green claim is not clear, not certain, and not commercially significant. A tool that exists at the margin, used once in a fraud of another kind, is not a greenwashing liability system.

H3: overlap is a strength only when definitions, evidence, and referrals are shared. Without that, overlap produces forum-shopping, orphan cases, and penalty-shopping. That is inchoateness, not productive redundancy.

I used secondary enforcement data because India has almost no public greenwashing case-file to code. That absence is both a limitation and a finding. Hohfeld is used for one job only: to stop us treating “corporate responsibility” as a moral cloud, and to ask who holds the claim-right that matches the duty.

Authors I push back on: (i) any account that treats greenwashing as only a marketing sin — that under-reads ESG-era claims; (ii) the view that Section 166(2) is already an adequate stakeholder duty — textually it is strong, operationally it is not. Bowen is right that this is symbolic environmentalism; I disagree that the answer can stay in social theory. It has to become liability design.

If rewritten today, I would add a short empirical annex: a coded sample of Nifty BRSR environmental claims against CPCB/consent records, and an update on the EU Green Claims Directive’s political stall and the SEC climate-rule withdrawal as cautionary transplants, not as models to copy blindly.

**If pressed — “so all three hypotheses were confirmed; is the study circular?”**

The hypotheses were framed from the gap in Chapter 1 and tested against the later chapters. Confirmation was not guaranteed. A finding that PFUTP already supplied deterrent securities liability, or that CCPA Guidelines were being used as binding rules with real fines, would have defeated H2. That is not what the materials showed.

## 6. Hohfeld, corporate personality, and limited liability

**Clubbed questions**

- Why Hohfeld rather than only fiduciary doctrine or law-and-economics?
- Hohfeld describes relations; he does not say who *ought* to have standing. Are you smuggling a reform into a description?
- Why the Realist–Stakeholder theory of the company?
- Are you attacking greenwashing, or attacking limited liability?
- Duty without a claimant: why open standing rather than public enforcement by MCA or SEBI?

**Answer**

Hohfeld is a diagnostic, not a manifesto. “Directors must protect the environment” sounds like a duty. Hohfeld asks: who holds the correlative claim-right, who has standing, and what happens if the duty is ignored. Applied to Section 166(2), the answers are: no clear external claimant, no reliable trigger, and a ₹5 lakh cap. That is how a strong sentence becomes an unenforceable relation.

The move from that description to standing reform is *lex ferenda*. I keep the two separate. Hohfeld tells us the duty is currently unmatched. Parliament or the courts must decide who should match it. My recommendation is one way to match it, not a deduction from Hohfeld.

I adopt a Realist–Stakeholder reading because that is the best fit for Section 166(2)’s text, which names members, employees, shareholders, community, and the environment in one clause. I do not need Gierke to rewrite Indian company law. I need a theory that does not treat the environmental limb as decorative.

I am not attacking limited liability. Limited liability is compatible with truthful disclosure. The problem is using the veil plus weak verification so that the firm captures an ESG premium while the public cannot test the claim. The target is unaccountable environmental *representation*, not the corporate form.

On standing: public enforcement by MCA and SEBI should be the first line. Open standing is the back-up because public enforcement has not occurred. If MCA and SEBI actually used their existing powers, the case for wide private standing would be weaker. I would still want a defined claimant class, not an unlimited actio popularis.

**If pressed — “Hohfeld is old American private law”**

The age is the point. It is a precise vocabulary for correlatives. I am not importing Hohfeld as Indian law. I am using it as a method to read an Indian section.

## 7. Section 166(2), Tata–Mistry, and Ranjitsinh

**Clubbed questions**

- Is 166(2) a co-equal environmental duty, or an enlightened-shareholder clause with stakeholder factors?
- Quote Tata Consultancy Services v. Cyrus Mistry. What did it say, and what did it not say?
- Ranjitsinh is a public-law climate-rights case. How does it bind private directors on ESG claims?
- You yourself list the limits of Ranjitsinh. Why still rely on it?
- Why are Sections 241–244 the wrong vehicle?
- How do you get past the business judgment rule?
- “Dead letter” is already in the literature. What is new?

**Answer**

*Lex lata*, Section 166(2) is an express statutory duty to act in good faith in the best interests of the company, its employees, shareholders, the community, *and* for the protection of environment. I read the environmental limb as co-ordinate, not subordinate, because the text does not rank those interests. I accept that many practitioners still treat it as a factor in a shareholder-centred decision. That is the enforcement reality. It is not the best reading of the words.

Tata Consultancy Services Ltd. v. Cyrus Investments Pvt. Ltd., (2021) 9 SCC 449, at paragraph 19.30, is used for a limited proposition: the environmental duty is a distinct limb of Section 166(2), not merely a subset of “community.” The case is not a greenwashing case. It does not create a private action for false ESG claims. I rely on it for statutory architecture, not for a ready-made cause of action.

M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India, 2024 SCC OnLine SC 570, recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change under Articles 14 and 21. It is a case against the State, about climate and a threatened species, not about BRSR. I do not claim horizontal application is automatic. I claim an interpretive effect: once environmental protection has that constitutional status, a court construing “protection of environment” in Section 166(2) cannot treat it as optional ethics. The judgment also speaks to directors needing to hold company interest together with environmental protection. That supports a stricter reading. It does not, by itself, decide a greenwashing petition.

I state the limits in the thesis because I am not loading the case. No remedial mechanism against private issuers; no holding on disclosures; the 166(2) link needs future cases. Those limits are why I recommend statutory standing and penalties rather than asking the Supreme Court to invent them.

Sections 241–244 are the wrong ordinary vehicle because they are shareholder tools with share-capital thresholds, designed for oppression, not for an external environmental claimant. A consumer or an affected community member is exactly whom the environmental limb names, and exactly whom 241–244 shut out.

Business judgment: I do not ask courts to second-guess a genuine climate strategy. I ask them to review *truth*. Whether a factory is “carbon neutral” is a verifiable representation, not a commercial hunch. Fraud, bad faith, and unsubstantiated public claims sit outside the protection of business judgment.

What is new is not the phrase “dead letter.” It is the demonstration that the same vacuum repeats in BRSR assurance and in CCPA guideline-status, and that greenwashing is the fact-pattern that makes the dead letter legally concrete.

**If pressed — “so there is still no case”**

Yes. No reported Indian decision applies Section 166(2) to an environmental misstatement. That is Finding 3. The duty is doctrinal; the pathway is missing.

## 8. The numbers: ASCI, CRISIL, 54%/47%, RBI, “zero enforcement,” fake EPR

**Clubbed questions**

- ASCI’s 100% is a pre-selected sample. Why call it systemic?
- Weak ESG scores are not greenwashing.
- Did you audit the 54% and 47% studies, or only cite the headlines?
- The RBI premium “if” does all the work.
- Define “significant” and “specifically” in the zero-enforcement claim.
- Are fake EPR certificates greenwashing or ordinary fraud?
- Are ASIC’s 47 interventions comparable to India’s director prosecutions?

**Answer**

I concede the fair statistical points, then keep the narrower claim.

ASCI 211/211 is not a census of all green ads in India. It is a 100% failure rate inside a set of environmental ads already taken up as potentially false. I should not say it proves that every Indian green ad is false. I can say that when the advertising self-regulator examined environmental claims, it did not find a clean subset. That is evidence of a substantiation problem in the ads that reach scrutiny, not a national prevalence rate.

CRISIL’s weak-ESG finding is *not* greenwashing. Weak performance is not a misleading claim. I use it only as context: a market in which most firms are weak on ESG still sells a large green-capital story. The greenwashing inference does not follow from the score. I will not defend it as if it did.

The 54% and 47% figures are other authors’ coding of listed-company ESG text. I did not re-code their sample. I use them as peer-reviewed indications of a persistent pattern among large listed firms, not as my own empirical result. If the panel wants prevalence proved by this thesis, that is outside the method. The thesis is about legal design.

The RBI ESG premium is also not proof of fraud. The sentence in the thesis is conditional: *if* a premium attaches to green labels, then false labels can extract unearned capital. That is a risk statement, not a finding that the premium is fake.

On enforcement, my defensible claim is this: as of the cut-off, SEBI, CCPA, and MCA had not imposed a public, named, significant *financial* penalty *specifically* for a misleading environmental *claim*. I do not say India had no environmental enforcement. ASCI modifications, NGT/CPCB action on about six lakh fake EPR certificates, Gensol–BluSmart, and a SEBI whole-time member flagging false BRSR claims all exist. Fake EPR is closer to environmental-market fraud than to an advertising green claim, but it shows the same verification deficit. Gensol is not a routine BRSR greenwashing prosecution.

Cross-country counts are not like-for-like if I compare India’s director prosecutions with Australia’s mix of warnings, infringement notices, and one large penalty. The fair comparison is visible regulatory intervention against environmental misrepresentation. On that comparison, India is near zero and ASIC is not. I will not pretend the 47 and the 90 are identical in legal character.

**If pressed — “then your opening is overstated”**

The opening uses strong illustrations to show scale and empty enforcement. The findings in Chapter 6 are narrower and I will stand on those.

## 9. CSR to ESG, BRSR, backsliding, and SEBI powers

**Clubbed questions**

- Why did the shift from Section 135 CSR to BRSR not solve the truth problem?
- Is “assessment or assurance,” deferred value-chain reporting, and the 75% cap capture, or proportionate burden-reduction?
- Why double materiality when ISSB is single materiality and the EU path is politically stressed?
- Gensol was a whistleblower case. Does that prove design failure?
- PFUTP and Section 15HA already bite on material lies. Why an express ESG clause?
- Companies pay their own assurers, like statutory auditors. Why is ESG different?
- Unlisted polluters are outside BRSR. Is expanding SEBI a jurisdictional overreach?

**Answer**

CSR measured spending. BRSR measures disclosure. Neither, by itself, measures truth. Finding 2 is that disclosure without verification, integrated oversight, and deterrent consequences does not close the gap between environmental language and environmental fact. Greenwashing has also moved *into* the disclosure documents that now look official.

I call the 2024–25 BRSR relaxations backsliding because they reversed the only two features that make disclosure costly to fake: independent assurance and value-chain visibility. Reducing compliance burden is a legitimate SEBI objective. It is not a greenwashing-control objective. Those two aims collided, and verification lost. I do not need to prove “capture” as a conspiracy. Design choice is enough.

Double materiality is a recommendation, not a description of current Indian law. I recommend it because greenwashing is often impact-side: harm to the environment that is not yet a P&L line. I accept that ISSB is the global investor-materiality path and that the EU model is under strain. For India I would phase it: keep financial materiality for all BRSR filers, add impact materiality first in high-impact sectors. I would not copy ESRS overnight.

Gensol shows that a serious case can be reached when insiders speak. A whistleblower-dependent system is not a monitoring system. Design failure is the absence of routine ESG surveillance, not the fact that one scandal was caught.

PFUTP can already reach a material BRSR lie. I still want an express clause for three reasons: it removes the argument that sustainability language is non-financial puffery; it forces referral to MCA for the director-duty limb; and it tells the market the rule before the first test case. It is clarification and coordination, not a new invention of fraud.

Assurance independence is the same structural problem as audit independence, with a worse market: thinner expertise, no equivalent of NFRA-level ESG oversight, and a standard that was diluted from assurance to “assessment.” ESG is not unique in theory. It is weaker in fact.

Unlisted polluters should not be pulled into SEBI. They belong to MCA thresholds and environmental regulation. My s.135A recommendation is deliberately MCA-side for that reason.

**If pressed — “SEBI was being practical”**

Practical for compliance cost, yes. Practical for claim integrity, no. Those are different tests. My thesis uses the second.

## 10. Comparative law: what can actually be transplanted

**Clubbed questions**

- Why EU, UK, US, Australia?
- The Green Claims Directive is unadopted. How is it “best practice”?
- The SEC climate rule was stayed and undefended. What lesson is that?
- Which two levers would you implement first, and which would you not transplant?
- Why will EU architecture not become more paper law in India?
- Australia’s 47 interventions: law, politics, or a specialist team?
- Turnover-linked 4–10% fines are a competition-law transplant. Why use them here?

**Answer**

I used those jurisdictions because they have the most developed anti-greenwashing *design variables*: substantiation, assurance, director duties, penalties, and multi-agency practice. Developing-country comparators appear, but they are not the design source. I am comparing regulatory architecture, not copying Europe as a moral model.

The Green Claims Directive is a proposal. As of early 2026 it is not adopted. I use it as a design lesson — pre-publication substantiation and accredited verification — not as Indian-ready law. The adopted EU instrument I rely on more safely is the Empowering Consumers / UCPD amendment that already restricts generic green claims.

The SEC episode is a lesson in political fragility, not a model to import. Climate-disclosure rules that depend on one regulator’s agenda can be switched off. India’s answer should be statutory hooks plus SEBI subordinate legislation, not a transplanted US rule.

First two transplants: (1) claim-specific substantiation with the burden on the company; (2) phased reasonable assurance plus mandatory cross-referral among SEBI, CCPA, and MCA.

I would *not* immediately transplant: EU-style pre-approval of every claim, full ESRS, or 10% global-turnover fines. Those need capacity and will drive greenhushing and MSME harm.

Architecture will not work in India if we copy text without people. That is why the general recommendations put a shared technical cell, market sweeps, and a lead-regulator protocol *before* the heaviest penalties.

Australia’s record is law plus political priority plus a specialist enforcement posture. India can copy the posture and the ASIC–ACCC coordination idea more easily than it can copy caseload numbers.

Turnover-linked fines are used because the harm scales with the size of the market deception, not because greenwashing is abuse of dominance. A ₹50 lakh cap is a budgeted cost for a large issuer. A small percentage of turnover is a different incentive. The number can be Indian — I use 1% first contravention and 4% subsequent in the CPA recommendation, not 10%.

**If pressed — “so you cherry-picked success stories”**

I also used the US as a *failure* of durable federal climate disclosure, and Canada as a warning on greenhushing. The comparison is not a victory parade.

## 11. Fragmentation, taxonomy, lead regulator, and penalty arbitrage

**Clubbed questions**

- Give one sentence that is lawful under one definition and unlawful under another.
- Is the 2025 Taxonomy a regulator, or are you over-counting?
- Many products already face five regulators. Why is greenwashing special?
- Who is the lead regulator?
- Parallel proceedings and due process?
- Have you ignored stock-price and reputational deterrence?
- If the real bottleneck is technical capacity, why add statutes?

**Answer**

Example: “Working towards carbon neutrality.” As puffery or an undefined aspiration, it may survive a loose advertising reading. As an investor-facing representation in a BRSR or green-bond document, the same words can be a misleading impression if there is no plan, no Scope coverage, and no offset quality. “Net zero” is also not a legal term in India. That is definitional fragmentation in one phrase.

The Green Taxonomy is not a new policeman. Calling it the “Green Taxonomy Paradox” means: India answered fragmentation with another voluntary classification instead of harmonising CCPA, SEBI, and MCA definitions. I count it as adding parallel vocabulary, not as adding a sixth enforcement agency.

Greenwashing is special because the *same sentence* changes legal identity as it moves from pack to ad to annual report to bond. Food safety does not do that. That is why I want a lead-regulator rule for this class of claims, not a new super-regulator for all products.

Lead regulator by channel, not one permanent emperor:

- Consumer-facing product/advertising claim: CCPA, with FSSAI or BIS in support.
- Listed-company disclosure, ESG rating, green debt: SEBI, with mandatory MCA referral if directors authorised it.
- Unlisted-company board failure: MCA.

A standing council coordinates; it does not replace. Referral is mandatory so a case cannot die because each body thinks it belongs elsewhere.

Due process: sequential or coordinated action, shared evidence, no double recovery of the same loss. Parallel investigation is acceptable; stacking punitive fines for the same facts without coordination is not.

Reputational harm is real and I do not ignore it. It is uneven, slow, and useless against a private company or a one-off campaign. Law cannot outsource deterrence to Twitter.

If capacity is the bottleneck — and Chapter 5 says it is — the first reform is a shared expert cell for LCA, GHG scopes, and offsets. Statutes without scientists will fail. Scientists without statutes will also fail, because they will have nothing binding to apply. Do both, in that order: capacity, then standards, then penalties.

**If pressed — “name one coordinating statute”**

I do not propose a new Greenwashing Act as the first step. I propose an inter-regulatory protocol with legal backing in SEBI regulations, CPA Rules, and an MCA circular, later raised into a statutory duty to refer. That is slower to write into one Act and more realistic.

## 12. Recommendations: defend the draft, not the wish-list

**Clubbed questions**

- 1% of turnover plus disqualification for a net-zero slogan — proportionate?
- Why is a director fined on company turnover?
- Open standing at NCLT: floodgates and strike suits?
- ESG committee with a science director, pre-approving all claims: talent, duplication, safe harbour?
- Scope 3 and reasonable assurance: cost and phase-in?
- Why Rules under Section 101 rather than a parent-Act amendment?
- Why 4%? Why green-leaf presumptions? Why bounties?
- What is the minimum viable package, in order?

**Answer**

I will not defend the recommendations as all-or-nothing, and I will not defend disqualification for a careless slogan.

Penalty: the 1% / ₹5 crore floor is for *materially misleading* authorised claims, not for a poorly worded aspiration. Disqualification should be reserved for persistent or knowing default, not a first negligent slip. If the panel finds disqualification too blunt, I will drop it from the opening amendment and keep the scaled fine.

Why turnover on a director: because the harm is scaled to the company’s market, and Section 166(7)’s ₹1–5 lakh is not a number that a listed-company director prices. I accept the fairness objection. A cleaner design is: company-level turnover fine, plus a separate personal penalty with a high but fixed ceiling, plus disgorgement if the director gained. I will take that as an improvement on my draft.

Standing: I would not leave “any affected person” unqualified. The viable version is: recognised consumer organisations, specified environmental NGOs, the Central Government, and members, with NCLT leave for others, plus costs against frivolous petitions. The Hohfeldian point is that *someone* other than a 10% shareholder must be able to sue. It is not that everyone should.

ESG committee: it is for listed companies and Section 135-threshold companies that make public environmental claims. Expertise can be a permanent invitee or a retained expert if independent-director supply is thin. It must *not* become a safe harbour. Board responsibility remains; the committee is an internal control, like an audit committee. Pre-approval is for material public claims, not every green leaf on a social-media post.

Scope 3 and reasonable assurance must be phased. My own phasing is the defence: top cohort first, then expansion; repeal the 75% cap once capacity exists, not on day one.

CPA Rules under Section 101 are faster than a parliamentary amendment of Section 21, and they can carry schedules for “carbon neutral,” “biodegradable,” “recyclable,” and “net-zero.” The parent-Act amendment is better for turnover penalties if the Rules route is thought too ambitious. Green-leaf imagery as a *rebuttable* presumption is about executional greenwashing; it is rebutted by showing the image was ornamental and no environmental claim was made. Bounties are optional and I will not die on them. Protected disclosure without a bounty is enough.

**Minimum viable package, in order**

1. Convert CCPA Guidelines into Section 101 Rules, with claim-specific schedules and a narrowed puffery clause.
2. Restore phased reasonable assurance for BRSR Core, and a SEBI–CCPA–MCA referral duty.
3. Give Section 166(2) a usable trigger: MCA referral from a SEBI or CCPA finding, plus a scaled penalty for *material* environmental misrepresentation.

That package already attacks H2 and H3. Committees, double materiality, bounties, and disqualification can wait.

**If pressed — “this is just more law”**

It is less law than a new Greenwashing Act. It is targeted amendment of three regimes that already exist.

## 13. Constitution, NGT, and “what courts can do tomorrow”

**Clubbed questions**

- Articles 21, 48A, 51A(g) bind the State. How do they reach private ads and BRSR?
- Is there a constitutional duty on regulators to enforce against greenwashing?
- Why not NGT as the primary forum instead of NCLT?
- IMA self-declaration of ads was diluted. What does that prove?
- What should courts do tomorrow, without Parliament?

**Answer**

Articles 48A and 51A(g) are directive and fundamental-duty provisions. They do not, by themselves, create a private cause of action against a company. Article 21, after Ranjitsinh, raises the interpretive baseline for *State* action, including how regulators and courts read environmental statutes. Horizontal application to private green claims is an argument, not current law. I use the Constitution as a backdrop for construing Section 166(2) and for criticising non-enforcement, not as a stand-alone writ recipe against every issuer.

I do not claim a free-standing constitutional obligation to run a greenwashing prosecution. I claim that once climate harm has Article 21 status, a regulator that has tools and does not use them is harder to justify.

NGT is the right forum for environmental harm, pollution, and fake EPR-type compliance fraud. It is the wrong primary forum for a misleading BRSR sentence or a director-duty claim. Those are company-law and securities-law disputes. Greenwashing straddles deception and environment; the *claim* is a market representation. NCLT and SEBI/CCPA fit that better. NGT remains complementary where the misrepresentation is tied to a concrete environmental contravention.

IMA shows two things: the Court tried to impose a pre-publication honesty certificate, and the executive narrowed it. That supports the backsliding theme. It also warns that courts are clumsy standing advertising regulators. That is why I prefer structured statutory rules to more judicial mandamus over ads.

What courts can do tomorrow: (i) treat a SEBI- or CCPA-proven environmental misstatement as relevant evidence of want of good faith under 166(2); (ii) read a material BRSR environmental untruth as a PFUTP misrepresentation without waiting for a new clause; (iii) refuse to classify measurable green claims as puffery. They cannot invent turnover fines or open standing. That needs the statute.

**If pressed — “so Article 21 does no work”**

It does interpretive work. It does not do remedial work against a private issuer. I will not sell it as more than that.

## 14. Greenhushing, MSMEs, independent directors, and over-deterrence

**Clubbed questions**

- Will this chill honest climate communication?
- Is this a listed-company thesis with universal recommendations?
- Will good people refuse board seats?
- India’s 2070 pledge is itself a long-horizon target. Are you applying to companies a standard the State fails?

**Answer**

Greenhushing is a real risk. I discuss it in the comparative chapter. The answer is proportionality: punish unsubstantiated *claims*, do not punish silence, and do not require pre-approval of every internal climate plan. Safe harbours for good-faith, dated, scoped, and planned targets reduce silence. A slogan without a plan is not the same as a cautious, qualified disclosure.

MSME and unlisted firms should not face BRSR-grade assurance. Consumer-law substantiation for claims they *choose* to make is enough: if you say “biodegradable,” prove it. If you say nothing, you owe nothing. The heavy architecture is for listed and large Section 135 companies that sell green claims into capital markets.

Independent-director flight is a reason to keep personal disqualification narrow and to insure reasonable investigation, not a reason to leave 166(2) unenforceable. The standard should be: you are liable if you approved a material claim without a reasonable verification process. You are not an environmental guarantor of all operations.

2070 is a sovereign political target. Companies that repeat it as *their* product or fund claim convert it into a market representation. The State is accountable politically and constitutionally; the company is accountable to the people who bought the claim. I am not putting the Union in the dock under Section 166(2).

**If pressed — “your penalties will cause greenhushing”**

Then calibrate penalties and keep qualified forward-looking statements safe if assumptions are disclosed. Do not abandon substantiation. Silence that is caused by fear of lying is still better than a false net-zero sticker.

## 15. Limitations, the best counter-argument, and close

**Clubbed questions**

- Which limitation most weakens which finding?
- If there is almost no Indian case law, how is this a critical study?
- Name the best argument against the thesis.
- If you had two more years, what empirical chapter would you add?

**Answer**

The limitations are stated in Chapter 1: law as of late 2025 / early 2026; secondary data; thin Indian precedent; no sectoral deep-dive beyond food/FSSAI.

The limitation that bites hardest is the absence of primary enforcement files. That most weakens any claim about *why* a particular regulator did not act — resources, politics, or design. I can show design defects in the statute and the circulars. I cannot prove motive. Finding 4, that the gap is mainly design, is therefore an inference from architecture plus comparative contrast, not from ethnography of SEBI.

Thin case law does not make the study uncritical. In a new regulatory field, criticism is of statutes, guidelines, circulars, and non-use. The empty docket is part of the material.

The best argument against me is this: India does not have an enforcement-design problem; it has a capacity-and-priority problem. Give CCPA and SEBI people, data, and political instruction, and PFUTP plus CPA Section 21 plus the 2024 Guidelines will be enough. Adding standing, turnover fines, and new committees will over-regulate, chill disclosure, and overload NCLT.

My reply: capacity is necessary and I recommend it. It is not sufficient, because the Guidelines are still guidelines, assurance was diluted by design, 166(2) has no claimant, and penalties remain capped below campaign spend. A well-staffed regulator still needs a usable legal trigger. I am willing to sequence capacity first. I am not willing to say the present triggers are adequate.

If I had two more years I would code a sample of BRSR Core environmental KPIs against publicly available pollution and consent data, and I would track every CCPA, ASCI, and SEBI action after October 2024. That would test H2 with Indian numbers instead of comparative contrast.

**Closing line if asked for last words**

The law already says directors must protect the environment and companies must not mislead. My thesis is about why those sentences do not meet, and how to make them meet without waiting for the next scandal.

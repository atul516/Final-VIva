# Chapter 4 — Comparative Tables: Anti-Greenwashing Regulation Across Jurisdictions

---

## Table 1: Jurisdiction-wise Greenwashing Regulations and Best Practice Features

| Jurisdiction | Main Greenwashing Regulations | Best Practice Features (Keywords & Phrases) |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **European Union** | • Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), 2023 <br>• Green Claims Directive (GCD) (Directive 2024/825, trilogue as of Jan 2026) <br>• Empowering Consumers for the Green Transition Directive (ECGT), Directive 2024/825, effective Sep 2026 <br>• Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD), as amended | • **Mandatory pre-publication approval** of explicit environmental claims by accredited independent verifiers (GCD); "Certificate of Conformity" issued before claim goes public <br>• **Third-party assurance** — mandatory limited assurance from Year 1 of CSRD reporting; pathway to reasonable assurance (audit-standard) by October 2028 <br>• **Double materiality assessment** — companies must disclose both how sustainability issues affect the firm *and* how the firm impacts environment/society <br>• **Lifecycle assessment (LCA) mandatory** — GCD requires robust, science-based LCA for any environmental product claim <br>• **Prohibition of generic or vague claims** — "eco-friendly," "green," "climate neutral" without specific evidence are banned under ECGT <br>• **Ban on offsetting-based "net zero" or "carbon neutral" claims** unless residual-only after maximum reductions <br>• **Turnover-linked penalties** — minimum 4% of annual turnover in the Member State + confiscation of revenues from misleading activity + temporary public procurement exclusion <br>• **Board-level accountability** — directors retain ultimate responsibility for accuracy of sustainability reports under CSRD <br>• **Convergent hard-law framework** — three interlocking instruments covering corporate reporting, product-level marketing, and consumer protection |
| **United Kingdom** | • FCA Anti-Greenwashing Rule (ESG 4.3.1R), effective 31 May 2024 <br>• UK Green Claims Code (CMA) <br>• Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act (DMCCA) 2024 <br>• Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations (CPRs) <br>• Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR) <br>• Companies Act 2006, s. 172 (directors' duties) | • **FCA "fair, clear and not misleading" standard** — claims must be substantiated with reliable evidence, complete (full lifecycle), and comparable only against genuinely similar products <br>• **Ongoing lifecycle compliance obligation** — substantiation is not a one-off exercise; firms must maintain compliance throughout product life <br>• **Turnover-linked fines** — CMA can impose up to 10% of global turnover for consumer law breaches including greenwashing, under DMCCA 2024 <br>• **Personal director liability** — Senior Managers Regime creates personal criminal liability for ESG disclosure governance failures <br>• **"Assumption of responsibility" doctrine** (Vedanta) — publishing a sustainability report creates a legal duty of care if stated processes are not followed <br>• **Derivative actions** — shareholders can sue directors for inadequate climate strategy (ClientEarth v Shell model) <br>• **Six-principle Green Claims Code** — truthful, clear, substantiated, full-lifecycle, fair comparisons, no misleading omissions <br>• **Sector-specific targeting** — CMA fashion industry guidance (Sep 2024) on misleading buzzwords |
| **United States** | • FTC Green Guides (1992, revised 2012; update pending) <br>• Securities Exchange Act s. 10(b) & Rule 10b-5 (SEC) <br>• SEC Climate Disclosure Rules (adopted Mar 2024; administratively stayed Apr 2024; withdrawn Mar 2025) <br>• State consumer protection statutes (class actions) | • **Claim-specific substantiation standards** — Green Guides provide detailed guidance for biodegradability, compostability, recyclability, carbon offsets, renewable energy claims; companies must possess adequate evidence *before* marketing <br>• **"Adequate and proper substantiation" standard** — companies bear the burden of proof for every environmental claim <br>• **Securities-law materiality approach** — environmental representations in SEC filings treated as material mis-statements subject to Rule 10b-5; personal officer/director liability for securities fraud <br>• **Private litigation as primary enforcement lever** — class actions (P&G Charmin, 2025; JBS USA, 2024) fill the gap where agency action is reduced or withdrawn  <br>• **No-offsetting-as-substitute principle** — "carbon neutral" claims without documented emission reductions face litigation risk <br>• **Fragmented regulatory framework, politically vulnerable** — no single federal greenwashing law; rules subject to judicial stays and executive withdrawal; state-level litigation fills gap |
| **Australia** | • Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) — misleading and deceptive conduct <br>• ASIC Act 2001 (Cth) <br>• Treasury Laws Amendment (Financial Market Infrastructure and Other Measures) Act 2024 — mandatory climate disclosure regime <br>• Australian Sustainability Reporting Standard AASB S2 (ISSB S2-aligned) | • **Active regulatory surveillance** — ASIC conducted 47 greenwashing interventions in 15 months (to June 2024): 37 corrective disclosures, 8 infringement notices, 2 civil penalty proceedings <br>• **Substantial civil penalties** — ASIC v. Mercer ($11.3 M penalty); ASIC v. Vanguard ($20 M penalty) established that misleading ESG fund disclosures carry serious financial consequences <br>• **"True to label" standard** — product names and claims must accurately reflect underlying substance; undefined terms like "socially responsible" or "ethical" without methodology are prohibited <br>• **Disclosure of methodology mandatory** — companies must explain how sustainability factors are integrated into investment decisions <br>• **Phased mandatory climate reporting** (commenced 1 Jan 2025): Group 1 entities from FY2025; Group 2 from FY2026; Group 3 from FY2027 <br>• **ISSB-aligned reporting standard** (AASB S2) — mandatory Scope 1, 2 and 3 GHG disclosures; scenario analysis including a ">2°C" scenario; TCFD-based governance, strategy, risk management disclosures <br>• **Personal director accountability** — directors personally liable for content of sustainability reports; ASIC adopts proportionate but active enforcement posture <br>• **Consistency requirement** — internal controls must ensure climate-related statements are consistent across all corporate communications |
| **Canada** | • Competition Act (as amended by Bill C-59, Royal Assent 20 June 2024) <br>• Competition Bureau Final Guidelines on Environmental Claims, June 2025 <br>• CVM (not applicable — this is Brazil; Canada uses Competition Bureau) | • **Greenwashing-specific amendments to competition law** — explicit prohibition of deceptive environmental claims in the Competition Act; first major jurisdiction to embed greenwashing standards directly in competition statute <br>• **Two-tier substantiation standard** — product claims: "adequate and proper test"; business activity claims: "adequate and proper substantiation in accordance with internationally recognized methodology" (IRM) <br>• **Affirmative burden of proof** — companies must substantiate claims *proactively*; burden is on the company, not the complainant or regulator <br>• **Private right of action** — unprecedented among major jurisdictions; businesses and consumers may bring applications to Competition Tribunal if it is "in the public interest" (effective June 20, 2025) <br>• **"Due diligence" safe harbour** — defence available if company took reasonable steps to prevent deceptive marketing <br>• **Substantial penalties** — up to CAD $10 M for first offence or 3% of annual worldwide gross revenues (whichever is greater); up to CAD $15 M for subsequent offences <br>• **"Greenhushing" risk acknowledged** — Competition Bureau guidelines explicitly recognise risk of chilling legitimate sustainability disclosure; proportionality and clarity built into enforcement guidance |
| **Brazil** | • Consumer Protection Code (Código de Defesa do Consumidor — CDC) <br>• CONAR Advertising Self-Regulatory Code, Annex U (effective late 2025) <br>• CVM Resolution 59/2021 (ESG metrics in stock exchange filings) <br>• Proposed Bill 2838/22 (national greenwashing taxonomy — pending) | • **Mandatory proof of environmental claims** — CONAR Annex U (2025) makes technical documentation or recognised certifications a prerequisite for any sustainability claim in advertising <br>• **Multi-actor enforcement** — Federal Public Prosecutor's Office, consumer defence agencies (PROCONs), National Consumer Secretariat, CONAR, and CVM all hold concurrent jurisdiction <br>• **Criminal liability for misleading advertising** — up to 1 year imprisonment for entities sponsoring or creating false environmental advertisements under the Consumer Protection Code <br>• **Significant administrative fines** — 2022 administrative action resulted in fine exceeding R$ 12 million (~€2.3 M) for ESG advertising violations <br>• **"Comply or explain" ESG disclosures** — CVM Resolution 59/2021 requires ESG metrics in filings; no mandatory assurance yet <br>• **Developing national taxonomy** — Bill 2838/22 proposes uniform taxonomy and enhanced penalties; not yet law |
| **Mexico** | • General Environmental Protection Laws <br>• Proposed amendment to Federal Criminal Code (greenwashing bill — pending) | • **Criminalisation of greenwashing** — proposed bill defines greenwashing as "false or simulated information on environmental or sustainability performance for benefit or profit" — a specific environmental crime <br>• **Custodial sentences** — proposed penalty of 2 to 10 years imprisonment for managers and executives <br>• **Corporate criminal liability** — legal entities themselves would face liability for environmental offences <br>• **Organisational due diligence obligation** — prosecutors required to investigate "failures in organisational due control," placing a positive legal expectation on companies to maintain robust ESG compliance systems |
| **Chile** | • Proposed Greenwashing Bill (pending before legislature) <br>• Consumer Protection Law (general) | • **Reversed burden of proof** — business must *prove* the veracity of its environmental claims in court (unlike most regimes where complainant bears initial burden) <br>• **Advertising ban as penalty** — convicted companies may be barred from making sustainability-related advertising claims for 1 to 5 years <br>• **Substantial fines** — up to USD 640,000 per violation <br>• **Goes beyond "misleading advertising"** — bill frames greenwashing as a distinct environmental wrong, not merely a marketing infraction |
| **Colombia** | • Decree 1369/2014 (environmental advertising verification) <br>• Consumer Protection Statute (general) | • **Mandatory verification of environmental advertising** — Decree 1369 requires prior substantiation of environmental claims in advertising <br>• **Fines under consumer law** for unsubstantiated or deceptive environmental claims |
| **Singapore** | • Consumer Protection (Fair Trading) Act (CPFTA) <br>• MAS ESG Disclosure Guidelines <br>• CCS Anti-Greenwashing Guidance (Oct 2025) | • **Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) jurisdiction** over ESG claims affecting securities transactions — fines up to S$250,000 or 7 years' imprisonment for false environmental claims influencing investment decisions <br>• **Five-principle substantiation framework** (CCS, 2025) — claims must be accurate, specific, not misleading by omission, verifiable, and fair in comparison <br>• **Phased GHG reporting** — Scope 1 & 2 mandatory from FY2025; Scope 3 from FY2026; assurance requirements to follow <br>• **SGX mandatory ESG reporting** for listed companies with external review |
| **South Africa** | • Consumer Protection Act (CPA) — s.29 (false, misleading, deceptive marketing); s.22 (plain language) <br>• Advertising Regulatory Board (ARB) <br>• King IV Code on Corporate Governance | • **Board-level ESG responsibility** — King IV mandates boards take "ultimate responsibility" for integrated ESG strategy, linking sustainability oversight to directors' statutory duty of care <br>• **ARB strict enforcement** — ruled against Ryanair for claiming to be "Europe's lowest-emission airline" without scientific substantiation <br>• **Integrated governance model** — sustainability claims, board accountability, and consumer protection law operate as a unified framework under King IV + CPA |
| **Japan** | • Financial Services Agency (FSA) ESG Disclosure Guidelines <br>• Green Growth Strategy (2020) <br>• Sustainability Standards Board of Japan (SSBJ) — IFRS S1/S2-equivalent standards (target March 2025) | • **ISSB-aligned disclosure standards** — SSBJ move toward IFRS S1/S2 equivalence raises bar for ESG claim accuracy <br>• **Enhanced ESG investment product labelling** — FSA guidelines reduce risk of misleading "green fund" classifications <br>• **Investor-driven accountability** — institutional investor pressure via Principles for Responsible Investment (PRI) fills the enforcement gap in the absence of specific greenwashing legislation |
| **Kenya** | • Kenya Green Finance Taxonomy (draft, April 2024) <br>• Consumer Protection Act 2012 <br>• Environmental Management and Coordination Act (EMCA) 1999 <br>• Advertising Standards Body of Kenya Code | • **Green Finance Taxonomy** — draft KGFT requires activities to demonstrate alignment with environmental objectives, "do no significant harm," and meet minimum social safeguards (ILO/OECD-aligned) <br>• **Constitutional environmental rights** — communities mobilise around constitutional right to clean environment, supported by international NGOs, creating litigation pressure even without a specific greenwashing statute |

## Table 2: Consolidated Best Practices in Anti-Greenwashing Regulation (Across All Jurisdictions)

| # | Best Practice | Description | Jurisdiction Exemplars |
|:--|:---|:---|:---|
| 1 | **Mandatory pre-publication / ex ante approval of claims** | Environmental claims must be verified and approved by an independent, accredited body *before* they are published or broadcast. This shifts the paradigm from reactive penalty to proactive prevention. | EU Green Claims Directive (Certificate of Conformity); Colombia Decree 1369/2014; India Supreme Court's SDC directive (*IMA v. UOI*, 2024) — narrowed to food/health |
| 2 | **Third-party verification by accredited bodies** | Verification must be conducted by independent, accredited conformity assessment bodies — not internal teams or commercially conflicted parties. | EU CSRD (mandatory limited assurance from Year 1); EU GCD (accredited verifiers); Australia ASIC (AASB S2); UK FCA (ISAE 3000 standard) |
| 3 | **Mandatory limited assurance (escalating to reasonable assurance)** | Sustainability data must be assured at least to "limited assurance" standard from the commencement of the reporting regime, with a credible pathway toward full "reasonable assurance" (audit-equivalent). | EU CSRD: limited assurance from FY2024; reasonable assurance by October 2028; Australia AASB S2: limited assurance from commencement |
| 4 | **Double materiality assessment** | Companies must report both on how sustainability issues affect the business (financial materiality) *and* on how business operations affect the environment/society (impact materiality). Single-direction materiality enables selective disclosure and thus greenwashing. | EU CSRD / ESRS; contrasted with single-direction materiality in ISSB S1/S2 (financial materiality only) |
| 5 | **Mandatory lifecycle assessment (LCA) for product claims** | Any environmental claim about a product must be backed by a full lifecycle assessment covering raw material extraction, production, use, and end-of-life disposal. Partial or "cherry-picked" lifecycle data is prohibited. | EU Green Claims Directive; FTC Green Guides (lifecycle consideration); UK Green Claims Code; Australia ASIC guidance |
| 6 | **Mandatory Scope 3 GHG emissions reporting** | Companies must disclose emissions from their supply chains (Scope 3), not just direct operations (Scope 1) and energy use (Scope 2). Excluding Scope 3 allows companies to claim climate progress while hiding their largest emissions source. | EU CSRD / ESRS E1; Australia AASB S2 (mandatory Scope 3); SEC rules originally included Scope 3 but withdrew under political pressure; HLEG "Integrity Matters" (zero tolerance for pledges excluding value chain) |
| 7 | **Burden of proof reversed onto the claimant** | The company making the environmental claim must affirmatively prove it is accurate. It is not for the consumer, investor, or regulator to disprove the claim. | Canada Competition Act (Bill C-59): "adequate and proper" substantiation required by the company; Chile proposed greenwashing bill (court burden on company); EU GCD (pre-approval burden on company); FTC Green Guides (substantiation before marketing) |
| 8 | **Affirmative claim-specific substantiation standards** | Generic substantiation is insufficient. Regulators specify *what* evidence is required *per claim type* — e.g., "carbon neutral" requires a complete lifecycle emissions inventory, retirement of verified credits, and disclosure of methodology; "biodegradable" requires testing under a named standard with timeframe and conditions specified. | FTC Green Guides; EU Green Claims Directive (LCA + verified methodology); UK FCA guidance (FG24/3); India CCPA Guidelines 2024 (mandatory substantiation but without claim-specific detail — identified as a gap) |
| 9 | **Prohibition of generic or vague environmental claims** | Terms such as "eco-friendly," "green," "sustainable," "natural," "clean," or "climate neutral" without specific, verifiable evidence are prohibited. Only claims tied to demonstrated, specific attributes are permitted. | EU ECGT Directive 2024/825; UK Green Claims Code & CMA guidance; FTC Green Guides; Australia ASIC Information Sheet 271; India CCPA Guidelines 2024; CONAR Annex U (Brazil) |
| 10 | **Ban on offsetting as a substitute for emission reductions in "net zero" / "carbon neutral" claims** | "Net zero" or "climate neutral" claims may not be based primarily on the purchase of carbon offsets. Offsets may only be used for *residual* emissions, after maximum feasible reductions within the company's own operations and value chain. | EU ECGT Directive (explicit ban); UN HLEG "Integrity Matters" (zero tolerance for offset-reliant pledges); KLM ruling (Amsterdam District Court, 2024) — "offsetting fallacy"; JBS USA NYAG litigation (2024) |
| 11 | **Turnover-linked, proportionate penalties** | Penalties must scale with the size of the company and the magnitude of the benefit gained from greenwashing. Fixed-rate or low-cap penalties are insufficient deterrents for large corporations (they become a "cost of doing business"). | EU: minimum 4% of annual turnover in the Member State; UK CMA: up to 10% of global turnover (DMCCA 2024); Canada: up to CAD $10M or 3% of worldwide gross revenues; Australia: unlimited civil penalties (case-by-case); contrast: India Section 166(7) Companies Act — max ₹5 lakh fixed fine |
| 12 | **Director/officer personal liability for ESG disclosure failures** | Senior executives and board members must face personal legal consequences — not just corporate liability — for authorising or failing to prevent misleading environmental claims. Corporate liability alone creates insufficient deterrence because the personal decision-maker is insulated from consequences. | UK: Senior Managers Regime (personal criminal liability); FCA AGR and Companies Act s.172; EU CSRD (directors retain ultimate responsibility); Australia AASB S2 (directors personally liable for sustainability reports); US: Rule 10b-5 personal liability for officer/director misstatements |
| 13 | **Codified fiduciary duty to oversee environmental disclosures** | Directors' environmental oversight responsibilities must be explicitly embedded in corporate governance statutes, not left to judicial implication. This creates a non-derogable governance-level accountability for the accuracy of sustainability claims. | UK Companies Act 2006, s.172 (interpreted by courts to include climate/ESG risks); EU CSRD (board-level sign-off); Australia AASB S2 (director responsibility); South Africa King IV Code; India — Section 166(2) Companies Act has the textual foundation but lacks enforcement architecture |
| 14 | **Private right of action / private enforcement mechanisms** | Private parties (individuals, consumers, NGOs, competitors) must have legal standing to challenge greenwashing claims directly before courts or tribunals without depending on under-resourced regulatory agencies. | Canada (Competition Tribunal private action, effective June 2025); US (class actions under state consumer protection law; FTC litigation model); UK derivative actions (ClientEarth v Shell); EU — primarily public enforcement model but consumer representative actions under ECGT |
| 15 | **Mandatory disclosure of ESG screening methodologies** | Financial institutions and investment funds must explicitly disclose the criteria, thresholds, and processes used to classify an investment as "ESG," "sustainable," "responsible," or "ethical." Silence on methodology makes ESG labels inherently misleading. | Australia ASIC (ASIC v. Vanguard; ASIC v. Mercer); Singapore MAS ESG disclosure guidelines; EU CSRD (ESRS disclosures on policies and methodologies); UK FCA Consumer Duty and COBS framework |
| 16 | **Consistency requirement across all corporate communications** | Environmental claims must be consistent across all formats — annual reports, sustainability reports, investor presentations, advertising, packaging, and websites. A company cannot apply rigorous standards to regulated disclosures while making loosely substantiated claims in marketing. | Australia ASIC RG 280; UK FCA FG24/3; EU Green Claims Directive (claims in all media); FTC Green Guides |
| 17 | **Safe harbours for good-faith disclosure ("comply or explain")** | To prevent "greenhushing" (companies going silent on sustainability to avoid liability), legal frameworks should provide clearly defined safe harbours for voluntary, good-faith disclosure that is transparently qualified and evidence-based. | Canada Competition Bureau Guidelines (2025) — "due diligence" defence; EU ECGT — EU Ecolabel as safe harbour; UK FCA — relies on qualifying language and documented evidence as mitigating factors |
| 18 | **Coordinated multi-agency enforcement architecture** | Greenwashing crosses consumer protection, securities, environmental, and company law jurisdictions. Effective enforcement requires coordination between agencies with automatic referral mechanisms and shared substantiation standards, rather than siloed jurisdiction. | EU (CSRD + GCD + ECGT operate jointly; Commission + national authorities + civil courts); UK (FCA + CMA + Trading Standards coordinating); contrast: India — CCPA / SEBI / MCA / MoEFCC operate without formal referral mechanism, creating regulatory arbitrage |
| 19 | **Recognition and regulation of "futurewashing" / unsubstantiated pledges** | Forward-looking commitments ("Net Zero by 2040," "carbon neutral by 2030") must be backed by documented, actionable implementation plans. A publicly stated pledge without interim targets, Scope 3 data, and a transition plan is a misleading omission of material fact. | UN HLEG (interim targets required for 2025, 2030, 2035); JBS USA NYAG litigation (2024) — no viable plan = fraud; EU CSRD (transition plans must be disclosed and updated every 5 years); UK FCA guidance |
| 20 | **Science-based, internationally recognised methodologies** | Environmental claims must be grounded in methods recognised by standard-setting bodies or regulatory authorities in multiple jurisdictions. Proprietary or un-tested methodologies do not satisfy substantiation requirements. | Canada Competition Act — "internationally recognized methodology" standard; ISO 14021 (self-declared claims); ISO 14064-3 (GHG quantification and verification); ISAE 3000 (sustainability assurance); ISSA 5000 (forthcoming global sustainability assurance standard) |

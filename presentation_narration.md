## Slide 2 — What Is Greenwashing?

So let us begin with the basics. What is greenwashing?

At its core, greenwashing is when a company presents itself as environmentally responsible without actually being so. It is the gap between what a company shows and what it hides.

On the surface, you see earthy packaging, green leaves on labels, terms like "100% Eco," "Natural," or "Recycled." You see self-awarded badges that look like official certifications but are entirely made up. You see high-budget PR campaigns that spotlight one small eco-feature while the company's core operations continue polluting heavily.

Behind that façade, the reality is different. Operations remain heavily dependent on fossil fuels, toxic emissions, and plastic waste. There is zero independent verification — no scientific proof, no third-party audits backing up these claims. And governance is completely disconnected — the marketing team runs these campaigns independently of what the boardroom actually oversees or decides.

So to put it simply — greenwashing is any misleading, exaggerated, vague, or unsubstantiated environmental claim made by an entity. It is not a harmless exaggeration. It is structured deception.

---

## Slide 3 — Why Greenwashing Matters To Us

Now, why should we care? Greenwashing is not a distant corporate problem. It hits us directly in three ways.

First, our wallets. When we deliberately pay extra for a product labelled "eco-friendly," we are spending our hard-earned money in good faith. But often, that premium does not fund genuine environmental solutions — it funds expensive PR campaigns instead.

Second, our choices. Millions of people adopt sustainable habits — carrying cloth bags, sorting waste, choosing conscious brands. But when companies make deceptive claims, these honest lifestyle choices become meaningless. Industrial pollution continues unchecked while consumers believe they are making a difference.

Third, and most importantly, our future. Corporate slogans like "Net-Zero by 2050" create a false comfort — a feeling that someone else is solving the climate crisis. This false security delays urgent action while extreme weather and toxic pollution continue to threaten our families.

So the problem in front of us is this: when every label claims to be green, how do we protect our wallet, our trust, and our family's future?

## Slide 4 — The Scale of the Problem: As Visible in India

Let me show you the scale of this problem with actual data from India.

ASCI — the Advertising Standards Council of India — reviewed 211 environmental advertisements in its 2024-25 Annual Report. Every single one of them required modification for lack of substantiation. That is a 100 percent failure rate.

Looking at listed companies — one study found that 54 percent of NSE-listed firms engaged in greenwashing. Another found 47 percent of NIFTY 50 companies were flagged. CRISIL found nearly 80 percent of 586 companies scored below average or weak on ESG parameters.

Now consider the money involved. ESG fund assets in India reached 1.2 billion dollars by March 2024, up from 331 million in 2020. Cumulative green, social, and sustainability-linked debt hit 55.9 billion dollars by December 2024. India is already the fourth-largest emerging-market source of sustainable debt globally. And RBI research shows that stocks making ESG claims deliver returns above market predictions — meaning there is a real financial incentive to make these claims, whether or not they are true.

And here is perhaps the most alarming data point — CPCB uncovered approximately 600,000 fake EPR certificates in 2024. The National Green Tribunal took suo motu cognizance of it. This tells us the verification deficit reaches even the infrastructure of environmental compliance itself.

At this scale, greenwashing is not harmless marketing hype. It distorts both consumer choice and capital allocation.

## Slide 5 — Research Gap

Now, existing scholarship largely treats greenwashing as a marketing or advertising issue. My research investigates something deeper — the structural flaws in legal accountability.

I identified five specific gaps. First, there is limited doctrinal work on invoking Section 166(2) of the Companies Act against misleading sustainability claims at the board level. Second, green claims are policed across siloed regulators — MCA, SEBI, CCPA, ASCI — creating jurisdictional overlap and an accountability vacuum. Third, mandatory ESG disclosure has expanded without mandatory third-party verification, leaving claims essentially self-certified. Fourth, distant net-zero pledges and questionable carbon-offset claims remain legally unaddressed. And fifth, there is very little doctrinal study on why regulators rarely bring deterrent enforcement actions against greenwashing.

This study addresses these gaps by treating greenwashing as a corporate governance failure — sitting at the intersection of company law, securities disclosure, and consumer protection — and proposes an integrated liability framework.

## Slide 6 — Research Methodology

The methodology used is comparative and analytical legal research, which is qualitative in nature.

For primary and secondary sources, I examined Indian statutes, judicial decisions from the Supreme Court, the NGT, and foreign courts, along with regulatory instruments like SEBI's BRSR, CCPA Guidelines, ASCI Guidelines, parliamentary reports, and research papers.

I used Hohfeldian decomposition to break down corporate duties into precise legal relations — rights, duties, privileges, and powers. I also employed principal-agent theory, legitimacy theory, stakeholder theory, decoupling theory, and institutional theory as conceptual frameworks.

For the comparative component, I conducted a cross-jurisdictional study covering the EU — specifically the CSRD and the Green Claims Directive — the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia, among others.

In terms of statutory interpretation, I applied textual, contextual, and purposive methods to analyse the Companies Act 2013, the Consumer Protection Act 2019, the Environment Protection Act 1986, and SEBI's LODR Regulations.

An important methodological distinction I maintained throughout is between lex lata — what the law currently provides — and lex ferenda — what the law should provide. The analysis and the reform proposals are kept separate.

Finally, I conducted a critical regulatory assessment, mapping design issues and coordination failures between MCA, SEBI, CCPA, ASCI, FSSAI, NGT, and BIS.

## Slide 7 — Hypotheses

The study tests three hypotheses, and they are mutually reinforcing.

The first hypothesis — the Accountability Failure hypothesis — states that greenwashing in India is primarily a consequence of weak corporate governance and the limited operationalisation of board-level environmental responsibility.

The second — the Liability Shield hypothesis — states that the existing legal framework does not impose sufficiently clear, stringent, and deterrent liability for misleading environmental disclosures, thereby enabling corporations to avoid meaningful accountability.

The third — the Regulatory Inchoateness hypothesis — states that the present legal mechanism involving different regulatory bodies is inchoate and incomplete.

These three are connected: governance-level gaps under H1 are amplified by non-deterrent sanctions under H2 and enabled by fragmented regulators under H3.

## Slide 9 — How Greenwashing Evolved and Why It Matters

Now let us look at how greenwashing has evolved over time and why that evolution makes it a much bigger problem today.

Starting with the definition — India's CCPA Guidelines define greenwashing as any deceptive or misleading practice involving concealing or omitting information, making vague, false, or unsubstantiated claims, or using misleading words and imagery that emphasize positive aspects while downplaying harmful ones. Globally, ESMA and Greenpeace define it more broadly — as sustainability communications that fail to fairly reflect the true environmental profile of an entity, product, or financial service. The core mechanism behind all of this is what scholars call organizational decoupling — when a company's public green commitments operate completely detached from its actual day-to-day operations.

Now, how has this evolved? At the product level, starting from the late 1980s, TerraChoice identified the "Seven Sins" of greenwashing — vagueness, hidden trade-offs, false seals, irrelevance, the lesser-of-two-evils trick, plus nature imagery and colour trickery on packaging.

At the corporate level, from the 1990s onward, it became about brand image makeovers and halo marketing — using PR campaigns to deflect attention from a company's polluting core business.

Today, it has moved into ESG and financial greenwashing. We see crosswashing — selective ESG PR that cherry-picks good metrics. We see reporting manipulation — companies omitting Scope 3 value-chain emissions. And we see futurewashing — unverified net-zero pledges that lack interim milestones.

The harm is multi-layered. Environmentally, it delays real decarbonisation and keeps high-emission businesses alive under transition slogans. Financially, it misdirects green capital — and in India, that is 55.9 billion dollars in ESG debt — and drives out genuinely green businesses. And in terms of market trust, it erodes investor and consumer confidence, especially when small fixed fines make greenwashing a cheap business gamble.

## Slide 10 — India's Legal Ecosystem for Tackling Misleading Environmental Claims

Let me now map India's existing legal ecosystem for tackling these misleading environmental claims.

The constitutional bedrock is strong. Articles 21, 48A, and 51A(g), combined with the landmark M.K. Ranjitsinh ruling, establish a solid duty to take environmental claims seriously.

The ecosystem rests on three pillars. Pillar One is Consumer Protection — the CPA 2019, CCPA, consumer courts, and ASCI. Its strength is broad jurisdiction over advertisements. Its limitation is that it is reactive, downstream, and consumer-facing only.

Pillar Two is Securities and Finance — SEBI, BRSR, BRSR Core, ESG debt rules, and the PFUTP regulations. Its strength is the formalized ESG reporting structure it has created. Its limitation is single materiality, limited assurance, diluted verification, and delayed third-party audits.

Pillar Three is Corporate Governance — the MCA and the Companies Act. Section 166(2) places environmental care inside directors' duties, and textually it is strong. But the limitation is narrow standing, fines capped at one to five lakhs, and zero reported cases.

There are also complementary laws — the EPA 1986, which provides environmental standards as objective benchmarks; the NGT with its suo motu and broad remedial powers; FSSAI with sector-specific jurisdiction; BIS and the Ecomark scheme, which remains voluntary; and the Trade Marks Act, which provides indirect protection by refusing registration of deceptive green trademarks.

## Slide 11 — Limitations of Consumer Law in Tackling Greenwashing

Now, an important question this thesis addresses is — why can we not just rely on consumer law to solve this problem?

There are seven clear limitations. First, consumer law is reactive by nature — it only intervenes after deceptive claims have already entered the market. Second, it focuses on individual harm — treating each case as a single consumer dispute rather than recognizing greenwashing as a systemic governance failure. Third, there is severe information asymmetry — the burden of proof falls on ordinary consumers who simply do not have access to technical data about a company's actual environmental performance. Fourth, consumer law has no boardroom mandates — it regulates ad copy, not the internal corporate systems or decision-making that produced the claim. Fifth, there is the puffery loophole — allowing scope for exaggerations creates a convenient escape for vague green buzzwords. Sixth, it imposes entity-only fines — penalizing the company budget rather than holding individual directors accountable. And seventh, the 2024 CCPA Guidelines are only guidelines — they may not withstand legal challenge as binding regulations.

The answer then becomes clear. Consumer law only polices the end-product advertisement. Corporate governance polices the internal systems, board oversight, and verified data that produced that claim in the first place. That is where the solution must lie.

## Slide 12 — Why Greenwashing Is a Corporate Accountability Failure

This slide addresses the jurisprudential foundations. Why should we treat greenwashing as a failure of corporate accountability?

First, what actually is a company? The thesis tests five classic theories of corporate personality, and they support the view that a company is a real social actor — its public green promises create real legal expectations. Under Indian law, this aligns closely with the stakeholder position: Section 166(2) makes environmental care an inherent duty of directors.

Second, I used Hohfeldian analysis to clarify the precise legal relations at play. There is a duty being breached — the duty not to mislead, owed to the State and to investors. There is a privilege being misused — managers may choose how to communicate, but that freedom stops at deception. There is power being held — companies control the ESG information that everyone else acts on. And there is immunity being retained — limited liability insulates those who benefit from a green reputation.

Third, I asked — to whom is this duty owed? A duty only matters if somebody can enforce it. Duties owed to the State are strongest because the CCPA and SEBI can act. Investor-facing duties are also enforceable but only through narrow disclosure channels. Section 166(2)'s environmental duty has no clear claimant — so it reads as an aspiration rather than enforceable law. That is the gap this thesis targets.

Fourth, why does the duty fail in practice? Because of information asymmetry — insiders know the true pollution levels; outsiders only see polished PR. And verifying these claims requires technical expertise that ordinary citizens simply do not have.

## Slide 13 — Why Section 166(2) Remains Weakly Enforced

Let me now explain specifically why Section 166(2), despite being textually strong, remains weakly enforced.

There are three categories of hurdles. First, substantive and doctrinal hurdles. The statutory terms "community," "environment," and "best interests of stakeholders" are broad and undefined. Courts hesitate to second-guess commercial decisions under the business judgment rule. And there is a precedent gap — no established case law connects environmental misstatements directly to a director's breach of duty.

Second, procedural and standing barriers. Mismanagement petitions under Sections 241 to 244 are open only to shareholders — external stakeholders like consumers, communities, or civil society are excluded. And critically, there is no referral bridge — when CCPA or SEBI find greenwashing, it does not automatically trigger MCA action under Section 166(2).

Third, remedial and economic deficits. Section 166(7) caps penalties at one to five lakhs — an amount that is trivial for any company engaging in greenwashing at a significant scale. And directors, facing no meaningful penalty, prioritize short-term commercial profit over environmental duties that carry no real enforcement risk.

The conclusion here is important: the duties already exist in Section 166(2) and SEBI rules. What is missing is the trigger — open legal standing, meaningful penalties, and an automatic bridge between regulators.

## Slide 14 — Limitations of SEBI's ESG Regime

Moving to SEBI's ESG regime — SEBI introduced BRSR and improved the ESG disclosure format, but the scope, assurance, and enforcement remain too weak to make greenwashing actually costly.

Let me trace the timeline. In 2021, BRSR became mandatory for the top 1000 listed entities. In 2023, BRSR Core added key KPIs, an assurance track, and value-chain reporting. But by December 2024, assurance was diluted and value-chain disclosure was deferred and made voluntary. By 2025, the scope was narrowed further even as the debt framework expanded.

There are clear design limits. BRSR covers only the top 1000 listed firms, leaving large unlisted polluters outside its reach. It follows a single-materiality focus, which means impacts on the wider environment stay under-reported. And there are no sector-specific metrics, weakening data comparability.

Then there is the backsliding problem. Mandatory audits have been replaced with a soft "assessment or assurance" option. Rollout delays leave most ESG data effectively self-certified. Value-chain reporting — Scope 3 emissions — has been deferred, made voluntary, and narrowed with a 2 percent threshold and a 75 percent cap.

And the enforcement reality tells its own story. The Gensol-BluSmart action was triggered by whistleblowers, not by routine ESG market scans. A SEBI Whole-Time Member publicly flagged false BRSR claims in January 2025. But SEBI still lacks a dedicated ESG enforcement team or any mechanism to cross-check corporate ESG claims against actual pollution data.

## Slide 15 — India Has Legal Grounds But Enforcement Is Weak

So let me summarize the Indian position. There is doctrinal strength in the law, but it has not yet been converted into visible, routine enforcement.

What is already in the law? Section 166(2) explicitly places environmental protection inside directors' duties. Supreme Court rulings — Tata-Mistry and Ranjitsinh — affirm environmental duties and climate rights. SEBI's BRSR and BRSR Core formalize detailed sustainability disclosures. The CPA 2019 and CCPA 2024 Guidelines prohibit deceptive green advertising.

But enforcement is practically absent. There are zero reported director liability cases under Section 166(2) for green claims. Narrow legal standing prevents affected public and civil society from filing claims. Penalties of one to five lakhs under Section 166(7) are treated as minor operational costs. And BRSR audits remain partial and delayed, leaving data largely self-certified.

To put this in comparative context — Australia carried out 47 interventions in 15 months and imposed an 11.3 million Australian dollar penalty against Mercer alone. The US FTC and SEC brought over 90 greenwashing enforcement actions between 2021 and 2023. The EU conducted coordinated sweeps, and France alone issued 141 warnings and 114 injunctions. India, despite having comprehensively written rules, has visible deterrent penalties close to zero.

## Slide 16 — Comparative Study: What Stronger Jurisdictions Do Differently

Let me now present the comparative study findings. What do stronger jurisdictions do that India does not?

I compared four dimensions across the EU, UK, Australia, and India. On board accountability — the EU and UK impose strong and direct accountability. Australia has growing and active enforcement. India has it written in law but rarely uses it.

On integrated architecture — the EU has integrated reporting and claim rules. The UK connects the FCA, CMA, and Company Law. Australia has active inter-agency teamwork. In India, MCA, SEBI, and CCPA work in silos.

On third-party audits — the EU is phasing in strict mandatory audits. The UK is expanding requirements. Australia is moving to mandatory audits. India has deferred and softened its audit requirements.

On future claims and offsets — the EU imposes strict limits on offset claims. The UK subjects them to regulatory checks. Australia actively polices them. India's regulation of these claims is weak and largely unregulated.

And on penalties — the EU links fines to company turnover. The UK allows up to 10 percent of global turnover. Australia has high civil penalty exposure. India still operates with low fixed-cap penalties.

## Slide 17 — Comparative Benchmark: 10 International Best Practices

From this comparative study, I distilled ten core design levers that effective jurisdictions share.

One — pre-approval for claims, requiring independent certification before any green claim is published. Two — mandatory third-party auditing, phasing from basic reviews to full financial-grade audits. Three — double materiality, disclosing both financial risks to the firm and the firm's real impact on the planet. Four — lifecycle assessment, requiring full cradle-to-grave analysis for eco-product claims. Five — mandatory Scope 3 emissions disclosure, covering the full supply chain. Six — reversed burden of proof, where the company must prove its claim is true rather than the consumer having to disprove it. Seven — specific rules for carbon claims, with strict standards for "carbon neutral" claims and verified carbon credits. Eight — banning vague buzzwords like "eco-friendly" or "green" without measurable proof. Nine — turnover-linked penalties, setting fines at 4 to 10 percent of total revenue. And ten — a connected multi-agency system with automatic case-sharing and joint investigations.

## Slide 18 — India's Enforcement Gap Is a Design Problem

This brings me to a key argument of this thesis — India's enforcement gap is not merely an implementation failure. It is fundamentally a design problem.

Stronger jurisdictions share common design features: proof before publishing, independent audits that escalate over time, clear accountability for decision-makers, turnover-linked penalties, and lifecycle coverage from product labels to financial filings.

India's regulatory design, by contrast, relies on reactive policing — authorities only step in after an ad has already run. Audits have been diluted — mandatory audits were postponed and replaced with soft checks. There is no pre-approval system — no mandatory third-party verification before claims are made. We have seen repeated regulatory rollbacks — delays and narrowed reporting scopes. And penalties remain fixed at low levels that do not deter large corporations.

This is a structural design deficit, and it needs structural reform.

---

## Slide 19 — More Faults Beyond Poor Regulatory Design

Beyond the design problem, there are five additional structural gaps.

Gap one is definitional fragmentation. CCPA, SEBI, and ASCI each define greenwashing differently based on their separate mandates. Key technical terms like "carbon neutral" and "net zero" remain legally undefined in India. This allows companies to invest in what I call "defensible wording" — carefully crafted language designed to survive legal challenge rather than tell the truth.

Gap two is regulatory proliferation and ambiguity. A single green claim can simultaneously touch CCPA, SEBI, MCA, FSSAI, and MoEFCC. The May 2025 Green Taxonomy added yet another parallel voluntary framework without harmonizing existing rules. There is no lead regulator, no mandatory data-sharing, and no joint investigative capacity.

Gap three is regulatory backsliding. SEBI diluted mandatory audits to optional assessment and deferred supply-chain reporting. MCA rejected parliamentary proposals for a dedicated ESG oversight body.

Gap four is reactive enforcement and technical gaps. The system is purely complaint-driven, and consumer cases take two to five years to resolve. Regulators lack specialized in-house expertise in carbon accounting and lifecycle audits.

And gap five is penalty arbitrage. CCPA can fine up to 50 lakhs, Section 166(7) caps at 5 lakhs, and FSSAI at 10 lakhs. These are all far below global benchmarks where the EU and UK impose penalties of up to 10 percent of turnover. Low fines turn greenwashing into an acceptable cost of doing business — essentially making it a rational business strategy.

## Slide 21 — Testing of Hypotheses

Now let me present how each hypothesis was tested and validated.

Hypothesis one stated that greenwashing is primarily a consequence of weak corporate governance. The validation examined four dimensions. On scope — greenwashing extends beyond consumer ads to investor-facing disclosures including BRSR reports, net-zero roadmaps, and corporate transition pledges. On duty allocation — consumer rules police ads, but corporate governance is the proper anchor for boardroom oversight. Hypothesis two stated that the existing framework does not impose deterrent liability. On scope — we evaluated whether legal costs exceed benefits across Company Law, Securities Law, and Consumer Protection. On duty allocation — duties exist, but ordinary liability routes are weak. Section 166(2) lacks open standing and consumer penalties lack deterrence. On enforcement pathway — weak verification and high proof burdens shield corporate violators. Legal duties without accessible triggers fail to create real liability. Conclusion — validated. The framework lacks deterrent liability.

Hypothesis three stated that the regulatory mechanism is inchoate and incomplete. On scope — greenwashing is a lifecycle phenomenon encompassing products, ads, corporate reports, ESG filings, and green bonds. On duty allocation — disconnected regulators create gaps and conflicting standards. On enforcement pathway — no lead agency, no mandatory case referrals, no shared data. Conclusion — validated. The ecosystem is fragmented and incomplete.

All three hypotheses stand formally validated.

## Slide 22 — General Suggestions for Regulatory Reform

Based on these findings, I offer two categories of reform proposals — general and specific. Let me start with the general suggestions.

First, institutional integration. We need a standing inter-regulatory council linking MCA, SEBI, CCPA, MoEFCC, CPCB, and BIS so that greenwashing findings by any one agency are automatically shared with all others.

Second, a lead regulator model. One coordinating agency should be designated with mandatory cross-referral and shared evidence protocols. This does not mean replacing existing regulators but creating a single point of coordination.

Third, standardised verification. All environmental claims should require lifecycle verification — LCA — product-category benchmarks, and accredited verifiers before publication.

Fourth, proactive surveillance. Instead of waiting for complaints, regulators should run regular market sweeps in high-risk sectors and cross-check ESG claims against actual pollution records.

And fifth, enforcement capacity. Regulators need shared in-house expertise in carbon accounting, GHG scope analysis, and ESG assurance. Without this technical capacity, even good rules cannot be enforced.

## Slide 23 — Specific Recommendations for Legal Reform

Now the specific recommendations, organized by the three key statutes.

Under the Companies Act and MCA — first, turnover-linked penalties. Section 166(7) should be amended so fines scale with company revenue rather than remaining at fixed one-to-five lakh caps. Second, expanded standing — affected stakeholders and civil society should be given standing to enforce Section 166(2) duties. Third, boardroom oversight — companies should be required to establish dedicated Board ESG Committees with environmental whistleblower protections.

Under SEBI and securities law — first, mandatory reasonable audits. SEBI should return to the time-bound path for full financial-grade ESG audits rather than the diluted assessment option. Second, full Scope 3 value-chain reporting should be restored — comprehensive supply-chain emissions reporting without loopholes. Third, fraud classification — material ESG misstatements should be explicitly classified as fraudulent under SEBI's PFUTP regulations.

Under CCPA and consumer law — first, binding regulations. The 2024 Guidelines should be elevated into formal binding rules under the Consumer Protection Act. Second, corrective advertising — companies should be required to issue public correction notices, with turnover-based fines for major deceptive claims. Third, the puffery exception should be narrowed — vague green buzzwords like "eco-friendly" or "sustainable" should not be allowed to pass as mere puffery.

The overall answer to research question seven is this: three statutes working together in sequence — substantiate before publishing, audit independently, refer cases automatically between agencies, and link penalties to turnover so that leadership carries the real risk.

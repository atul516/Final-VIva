📜 Genesis & Etymological Trajectory

  • Jay Westerveld (1986): Coined at Beachcomber Resort (Fiji) / Gramercy Park Hotel context; observed hotels urging towel reuse ostensibly for ecology while cutting operational laundering costs without real conservation intent.
  • OED Definition (1999): "Disinformation disseminated by an organisation so as to present an environmentally responsible public image."
  • Corporate Evolution: Shifted from retail consumer puffery (1990s) to executive sustainability reports, ESG fund ratings, and sovereign green debt credentials.

⚖️ Competing Definitions (Thesis 2.1.2)

  • Oxford Dictionary (1999): "Disinformation disseminated by an organisation so as to present an environmentally responsible public image."
  • Greenpeace: A PR tactic that makes a company or product appear environmentally friendly "without meaningfully reducing its environmental impact."
  • Webster's New Millennium: Promoting environmentally friendly programmes to deflect attention from less savoury activities.
  • ESMA: Sustainability statements, actions or communications that do not clearly and fairly reflect the underlying sustainability profile of an entity or product, misleading consumers or investors.
  • CCPA Guidelines (India, 2024): Deceptive, vague, false or unsubstantiated environmental claims across marketing, packaging or disclosure.
Critical crux of § 2.1

The common thread across definitions is the gap between claim and reality — through falsehood, vague language, missing context, or selective disclosure. Not every case begins as deliberate fraud; some arise from weak controls and loose sustainability language. The result is the same: a distorted picture of environmental performance. Greenwashing is also not confined to corporations — governments, public institutions and NGOs may use the same legitimacy tactic.

Chapter 2 Section 2.2 Theoretical Frameworks Institutional Theory

Theoretical Frameworks for Understanding Greenwashing

Deconstructing the structural motives behind corporate greenwashing through four foundational socio-economic theories.

🏛️ 1. Legitimacy Theory

  • Core Premise: Corporations operate under an implicit social contract and must appear congruent with societal environmental norms (Suchman, 1995).
  • Greenwashing Mechanism: When environmental performance lags behind societal expectations, firms deploy symbolic actions rather than substantive operational changes to preserve their societal license to operate.
  • Lindblom's 4 Legitimacy Strategies: Educate stakeholders, change stakeholder perceptions without changing performance, manipulate attention, or alter external expectations.

👥 2. Stakeholder Theory & External Pressures

  • Core Premise: Corporate survival depends on satisfying competing stakeholder demands (Freeman, 1984).
  • Greenwashing Mechanism: Conflicting demands between shareholder profit maximization and civil society ecological demands create structural pressure to fabricate low-cost green signals.
  • Selective Disclosure: Highlighting minor pro-social initiatives (e.g. tree planting) to placate vocal stakeholders while concealing polluting core operations.

🔄 3. Institutional Theory & Isomorphism

  • Core Premise: Organizations adopt structures and practices to gain institutional legitimacy and conformity within their organizational field (DiMaggio & Powell, 1983).
  • Three Isomorphic Pressures:
    • Coercive: Complying superficially with government ESG reporting mandates.
    • Mimetic: Copying industry leaders' ESG buzzwords during times of uncertainty.
    • Normative: Adopting sustainability certification jargon promoted by consultants.

🪞 4. Decoupling Theory (Symbolic vs Substantive)

  • Meyer & Rowan (1977) Framework: Formal corporate structures are decoupled from day-to-day technical activities to maintain external legitimacy while preserving operational efficiency.
  • Means-Ends Decoupling: Policies are fully implemented and audited, but lack any causal connection to actual environmental outcomes.
  • Governance Implication: Standard disclosure regulations fail if they only audit policy existence rather than verifiable ecological outcomes.
Critical crux of § 2.2

Frances Bowen's social energy penalty captures the social cost when powerful firms manage criticism through symbolic environmental gestures instead of substantive reform. Once legitimacy can be purchased through image, deceptive claims become a rational tactic, not a communicative lapse. Delmas & Burbano (2011) show that external stakeholder pressure, internal incentives and regulatory uncertainty jointly drive the practice. Stakeholder fragmentation also explains brownwashing — understating real progress to avoid later accusations of hypocrisy.

Chapter 2 Section 2.3 Typology & Classifications Seven Sins

Typology and Classification of Greenwashing

A multi-layered taxonomy categorizing greenwashing practices from retail product claims to macro corporate and ESG financial instruments.

📦 Product-Level

Focused on direct consumer marketing, packaging, and advertising claims.

  • Hidden Trade-Off: Claiming 'energy-efficient paper' while ignoring deforestation.
  • No Proof: Eco-assertions lacking verifiable lab data.
  • Vagueness: Meaningless terms like 'eco-friendly', 'all-natural'.
  • Worshiping False Labels: Creating self-certified fake certification seals.
  • Lesser of Two Evils: 'Organic cigarettes' or 'green pesticides'.

🏢 Corporate-Level

Institutional branding and executive positioning of entire business models.

  • Executional Greenwashing: Using imagery (green leaves, clean rivers, chirping birds) to subtly cue environmental virtue.
  • Selective Disclosure: Showcasing 2% clean energy investments while allocating 98% capex to fossil extraction.
  • Unrealistic Transition Plans: Pledging Net-Zero 2050 with no interim milestones or capital allocation.

📈 ESG & Financial

Misleading capital market actors and investment fund beneficiaries.

  • Fund Mislabeling: Selling funds as 'Dark Green' (Article 9 SFDR) when holding heavy polluters.
  • Unsubstantiated Offsets: Purchasing cheap, non-additionality carbon credits to claim carbon neutrality.
  • Benchmark Arbitrage: Cherry-picking rating methodologies to inflate ESG scores.

Consequences of Greenwashing

Examining the tri-partite damage caused by deceptive corporate environmental claims across markets, ecosystems, and investor confidence.

🌍 1. Ecological & Societal Harm

  • Opportunity Cost of Inaction: Greenwashing creates an illusion of progress, delaying essential state regulation and direct decarbonization capex.
  • Accelerated Resource Depletion: Consumers purchasing 'eco' products consume more under the 'moral licensing' psychological effect.
  • Subsidizing Pollution: Polluting enterprises continue operations unhindered while enjoying enhanced brand prestige.

📊 2. Capital & Market Distortion

  • Akerlof's Market for Lemons: When consumers cannot distinguish real green products from greenwashed ones, willingness to pay drops, driving genuine sustainable innovators out of the market.
  • Capital Misallocation: Trillions in ESG investment funds flow to deceitful firms rather than true decarbonization technologies.
  • Unfair Competition: Deceptive firms free-ride on sustainability premiums without incurring the genuine costs of cleaner production.

🛡️ 3. Cynicism & Trust Collapse

  • Green Fatigue: Consumers overwhelmed by conflicting claims disengage entirely from sustainable purchasing.
  • Greenhushing Backlash: Authentic innovators stop publicizing genuine environmental breakthroughs to avoid being accused of greenwashing.
  • Erosion of Disclosure Legitimacy: Voluntary sustainability reporting becomes discredited as mere public relations.

Chapter 2 Section 2.6 Corporate Governance Doctrinal Imperative

The Case for Corporate Governance Intervention

Why ex-post consumer protection laws are structurally insufficient and why greenwashing must be anchored in board oversight and fiduciary liability.

⚖️ Limitations of Consumer Protection Law (CPA 2019)

  • Ex-Post & Reactive: Consumer law operates only after deception has occurred, harm is inflicted, and a complaint is formally filed.
  • Low Penalty Ceiling: CCPA Section 21 penalties (₹10–50 Lakhs) are negligible compared to multi-crore branding windfalls ('penalty arbitrage').
  • Excludes Capital Markets: Consumer protection law does not govern ESG debt issuances, institutional investor disclosures, or board oversight duties.

🏛️ Corporate Governance as the Doctrinal Anchor

  • Root Cause Intervention: Greenwashing decisions are made in executive suites and boardrooms, driven by executive compensation tied to ESG metrics and share price targets.
  • Fiduciary Duty Connection: Misleading disclosures represent a breach of the board's duty of care, oversight, and good faith.
  • Preventative Internal Controls: Requiring mandatory board-approved ESG substantiation before public dissemination eliminates deceptive claims at the source.
📜 CCPA Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Greenwashing (October 2024)
Issued under Section 18 of CPA 2019. Mandates that green claims must be substantiated with reliable, verifiable scientific evidence, clear comparative baselines, and qualified statements. However, its institutional reach is confined to consumer transactions, leaving capital market ESG disclosures governed separately by SEBI.

⚠️ Structural Limits (Thesis § 2.6.3)

  • Downstream only: The architecture is consumer-facing and advertisement-oriented; it does not create board-level verification duties or director accountability.
  • No ex-ante internal controls: Substantiation is required after a claim is made, not before it enters the market.
  • Puffery carve-out: Leaves interpretive room for aspirational environmental messaging and long-horizon transition narratives to escape scrutiny.
  • Upstream alternative: Comparative regimes treat greenwashing as a governance failure and place responsibility on boards before publication (OECD Regulatory Policy Outlook 2025: proactive, risk-based supervision).

🎯 Core Synthesis & Doctrinal Trajectory

Chapter 2 has established that greenwashing is no longer an isolated advertising irregularity but a systemic structural failure of corporate governance. Theoretical frameworks (Legitimacy, Stakeholder, Institutional, and Decoupling theories) demonstrate that companies are structurally incentivized to deploy low-cost symbolic green signals when the cost of substantive decarbonization is high and verification mechanisms are weak.

Chapter 3 Section 3.1 Corporate Jurisprudence Personality Theories

Jurisprudential & Theoretical Frameworks of Corporate Responsibility

Exploring how classical and contemporary theories of corporate personality justify legal accountability for environmental representations.

🎭 1. Fiction & Concession Theory

Pope Innocent IV; Savigny; Salmond

  • The corporation exists solely by state grant/concession.
  • Because limited liability and legal personhood are privileges granted by the sovereign, the state possesses inherent authority to condition corporate existence upon public interest and ecological protection.

🌐 2. Realist / Organic Theory

Gierke; Maitland

  • The corporation is a living social organism with a real will and distinct institutional identity.
  • As an autonomous actor exercising immense societal power, the corporation bears independent moral and legal responsibility for ecological degradation and deceptive green representations.

📑 3. Contractual / Nexus of Contracts

Coase; Jensen & Meckling; Easterbrook & Fischel

  • Corporation is a nexus of voluntary contracts between shareholders, employees, creditors, and consumers.
  • Greenwashing constitutes fraudulent misrepresentation breaching the explicit and implicit terms of consumer and investor contracts.

🎯 4. Purpose Theory (Brinz & Duguit)

Corporate assets are property dedicated to a specific socio-economic purpose. Modern societal consensus requires this purpose to incorporate sustainable development and intergenerational equity rather than unconstrained ecological exploitation.

👥 5. Stakeholder Theory (Freeman, 1984)

The board owes duties not exclusively to residual equity holders, but to all constituents who affect or are affected by corporate actions, including local communities, ecosystems, and future generations.

Synthesis of personality theories (§ 3.1.6)

Every classical theory can justify environmental accountability — concession (state may condition the privilege of incorporation), realism (the firm is an autonomous moral actor), purpose (assets are dedicated to a social end), contract (greenwashing is misrepresentation) and stakeholder (affected constituencies count). Section 166(2) is best read as the statutory expression of that synthesis, not as a novel philanthropic add-on.

Chapter 3 Section 3.3 Hohfeldian Analysis Analytical Jurisprudence

Jurisprudential Analysis Through Hohfeldian Principles

Decomposing corporate environmental obligations into precise jural correlatives and opposites (Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld, 1913).

The Hohfeldian Jural Matrix Applied to Greenwashing Analytical Matrix

Table 1: Jural Correlatives (Vertical Pairs)

• Right (Claim) ⟷ Duty: Public/Consumer Right to truthful ecological data imposes a strictly enforceable Legal Duty on corporations not to greenwash.
• Privilege (Liberty) ⟷ No-Right: Corporation has a privilege to adopt voluntary green branding only where it does not infringe public rights.
• Power ⟷ Liability: Regulators (SEBI/CCPA) hold the Power to alter corporate legal relations by sanctioning greenwashing, creating corporate Liability.
• Immunity ⟷ Disability: Public holds Immunity from being bound by unverified green claims.

Table 2: Jural Opposites (Diagonal Pairs)

• Right vs. No-Right: Absence of a right to deceive.
• Privilege vs. Duty: A duty not to greenwash destroys any commercial privilege of advertising puffery.
• Power vs. Disability: Lack of power to self-certify compliance.
• Immunity vs. Liability: Inability of corporations to escape statutory penalties.

🔬 Why Hohfeldian Analysis Solves the Greenwashing Problem

Corporate rhetoric often treats environmental responsibility as a Privilege / Liberty (voluntary altruism). Hohfeldian rigor proves that under Section 166(2) of the Companies Act 2013, environmental protection is a strict Duty whose jural correlative is a public/state Claim-Right. Deceptive green claims represent an illegitimate usurpation of privilege where a strict legal duty exists.

⚠️ Why a Duty Still Becomes a "Dead Letter"

  • Starting point is managerial privilege: Corporate law begins with discretion, not duty. Privilege means others have a no-right to stop the act — it does not create a duty of cooperation on shareholders.
  • Standing gap: Hohfeld asks: which real person can enforce Section 166(2)? In practice, environmental stakeholders have no direct claim-right; action depends on the company, MCA or SEBI.
  • Revlon contrast: Revlon, Inc. v. MacAndrews & Forbes (Del. 1986) shows how a duty becomes enforceable only when the decisional space is narrow and the criterion (best price) is verifiable. Environmental protection lacks that trigger unless statute supplies standing and a standard.
  • Limited liability as investor immunity: Shareholders are shielded from full personal exposure; the correlative disability sits with victims and creditors unless directors are separately made liable.

Chapter 3 Section 3.4 Agency Theory Information Economics

Principal-Agent Theory & Information Asymmetry

Modeling corporate greenwashing as an agency problem between corporate managers (agents) and shareholders/stakeholders (principals).

👥 The Tri-Partite Agency Conflict

  • Manager vs. Shareholder: Executives exaggerate ESG performance to hit short-term performance bonuses tied to sustainability ratings, exposing the company to long-term litigation and reputational destruction.
  • Majority vs. Minority Shareholders: Controlling promoters use greenwashing PR to distract from self-dealing or high-carbon related-party transactions.
  • Company vs. Society: Corporation externalizes ecological degradation costs while capturing private green pricing premiums.

📉 Information Asymmetry & Adverse Selection

  • Hidden Information (Pre-Contractual): Consumers and ESG fund managers face exorbitant screening costs to verify whether a 'carbon-neutral' claim is backed by real emissions reductions.
  • Hidden Action (Post-Contractual Moral Hazard): Once capital or customer loyalty is secured, management slashes substantive green capex while maintaining marketing claims.
  • Signaling Failure: In the absence of mandatory third-party reasonable assurance, low-quality greenwashers mimic authentic high-cost green signals.

Chapter 3 Section 3.5 Indian Regulatory Evolution CSR to ESG Transition

From Voluntary CSR to Mandatory ESG: The Indian Transition

Analyzing India's shift from voluntary corporate philanthropy to mandatory Section 135 CSR spending and SEBI's BRSR Core disclosure architecture.

📜 Section 135 CSR Regime

  • Mandatory Spend: 2% of average net profits for qualifying companies (Net Worth ≥ ₹500 Cr, Turnover ≥ ₹1000 Cr, Net Profit ≥ ₹5 Cr).
  • Input-Oriented: Regulates spend rather than operational environmental impact; allows companies to spend on CSR while polluting via core business.

📊 SEBI BRSR Core Framework

  • Business Responsibility & Sustainability Reporting (BRSR): Mandatory for top 1000 listed entities from FY 2022-23.
  • BRSR Core (2023): Key performance indicators (GHG footprint, water intensity, circularity) requiring reasonable assurance.

🔍 The Assurance Gap

  • Limited vs. Reasonable: Most BRSR filings use limited assurance (negative confirmation: 'nothing has come to our attention') rather than reasonable assurance (rigorous positive verification).
  • Value Chain Blindspot: Scope 3 value chain disclosures remain voluntary or delayed.
Critical assessment (§ 3.5.4–3.5.5)

The shift from Section 135 spend to BRSR disclosure increased volume and formality of sustainability reporting, but neither model guarantees truth. CSR regulates expenditure, not core operations. BRSR is more relevant to modern greenwashing, yet assurance is phased in only for Core indicators, companies choose and pay their own assurers, large unlisted companies remain outside, and the template uses single materiality with no uniform accounting methodology. Result: greenwashing migrated from advertisements into reports that carry greater institutional credibility.

Chapter 3 Section 3.6 Fiduciary Duties Section 166(2)

Fiduciary Duties & Environmental Accountability Under Indian Law

Doctrinal analysis of Section 166(2) of the Companies Act, 2013 establishing directors' fiduciary duty to protect the environment.

⚖️ Section 166(2), Companies Act, 2013 (The Doctrinal Crown Jewel)
"A director of a company shall act in good faith in order to promote the objects of the company for the benefit of its members as a whole, and in the best interests of the company, its employees, the shareholders, the community and for the protection of environment."

🏛️ Doctrinal Significance of Section 166(2)

  • Pluralist Corporate Model: Explicitly rejects Anglo-American shareholder primacy (Enlightened Shareholder Value); elevates the environment as a co-equal, independent fiduciary beneficiary.

Chapter 4 Section 4.8 Landmark Case Laws Judicial Precedents

Landmark Enforcement Actions & Judicial Reasoning

In-depth case studies of global judicial rulings establishing liability for corporate greenwashing — including the thesis's foundational automotive and 2025 consumer-litigation precedents.

India contrast (thesis closing note of § 4.8)

No Indian court, tribunal or regulator has produced a comparable greenwashing-specific ruling or penalty. Indian companies make claims of similar ambition — net-zero pledges, carbon-neutrality assertions, sustainable-sourcing narratives — yet face none of the scrutiny seen in Volkswagen, Shell, KLM, JBS, Vanguard and Mercer.

1. Volkswagen "Dieselgate" (2015– ) — foundational precedent
Software to falsify emissions tests on 11 million vehicles; over US$ 32 billion in global compensation. English courts, after a preliminary issues trial, held EA189 engines contained prohibited defeat devices, adopting German KBA findings. Principles: (i) regulatory findings bind later courts; (ii) liability extends beyond intentional fraud at the point of sale; (iii) damages can be existential. Volkswagen India (2019) is the domestic echo listed in the thesis case table.
2. Daimler Mercedes Emissions Settlement (2022)
US settlement of US$ 1.5 billion plus European settlements over defeat-device misrepresentations. Defence that Mercedes technology "differed from Volkswagen" was rejected. Showed automotive greenwashing was industry-wide, not a one-firm scandal.
3. Fossielvrij NL v. KLM (Amsterdam District Court, March 2024)
First case worldwide to successfully challenge aviation's sustainability narrative. 15 statements in "Fly Responsibly" held misleading. Three findings: (i) "fly CO2ZERO" / "becoming sustainable" is an unqualified, overly rosy picture; (ii) offsetting fallacy — no direct link between a passenger's reforestation payment and that flight's CO2; (iii) SAF cannot be marketed as a "solution" while its share of fuel remains tiny. No fine (ads already withdrawn); declaratory judgment + costs. Court: KLM could have "reasonably foreseen" consumers would be misled.
4. ASIC v. Vanguard Investments Australia Ltd (FCA, 2024)
Holding: Vanguard misled investors regarding its Ethically Conscious Global Aggregate Bond Index Fund by claiming it screened out fossil fuel issuers, when in fact 46% of the portfolio was not screened against ESG criteria. Fined AUD 12.9 Million. ESG screening methodology itself is a verifiable representation.
5. ASIC v. Mercer Superannuation (Federal Court of Australia, August 2024)
Holding: Mercer made misleading statements about Sustainable Plus investment options, claiming exclusion of companies involved in fossil fuels, alcohol, and gambling, while holding 15 fossil fuel and 15 alcohol companies. Fined AUD 11.3 Million.
6. People of the State of New York v. JBS USA Food Co. (New York Supreme Court, Feb 2024)
Allegations: NY Attorney General sued world's largest meat processor for marketing "Net Zero by 2040" commitments while planning major production expansions, lacking Scope 3 baseline calculations, and having no feasible execution pathway.
7. Milieudefensie et al. v. Royal Dutch Shell plc (The Hague, 2021 / appeal 2024)
Operative ground: Article 6:162 Dutch Civil Code — "unwritten standard of care" — read with Arts. 2 and 8 ECHR and the Paris Agreement, but not the ECHR itself. District Court ordered 45% net CO2 cut by 2030 vs 2019. The court treated the gap between Shell's published climate commitments and actual operations as evidence of breach (classic policy–practice decoupling). November 2024 appeal: duty of care to combat dangerous climate change upheld; the specific 45% number overturned because courts cannot prescribe a precise reduction target for an individual company.
8. Keurig Canada Inc. (Competition Bureau, 2022)
CAD 3 million penalty for claiming single-use K-Cup pods were recyclable in municipal programmes without disclosing widespread sorting limitations. Recyclability is a claim of present fact, not aspiration.
9. P&G Charmin Greenwashing Claim (US class action, January 2025)
Alleged false "sustainably sourced" marketing despite pulp from Canadian boreal forests via clear-cutting. Complaint: (i) FSC and Rainforest Alliance logos used when only a fraction of pulp is certified; (ii) "Keep Forests as Forests" / "Protect-Grow-Restore" branding inconsistent with sourcing; (iii) violation of FTC Green Guides ban on unqualified general environmental-benefit claims. Thesis point: non-binding guidance can still shape private litigation when agency enforcement is weak — a pathway India has not generated.

📜 CCPA Guidelines (in force 15 Oct 2024)

  • Scope: Claims about goods, services, manufacturing, process, packaging, use or disposal. Bind manufacturers, service providers, traders, agencies and endorsers.
  • Mandatory substantiation: Verifiable evidence — independent studies or third-party certifications.
  • Generic terms banned unless qualified: "clean", "green", "eco-friendly", "carbon-neutral", "natural", "pure".
  • Adequate disclosures: Material facts in the ad itself or via QR / URL. Cherry-picking favourable data while hiding adverse data is a violation.
  • Endorsers: Can be barred from appearing in advertisements for 1–3 years.

⚠️ Three Structural Limits of the Guidelines

  • Not binding subordinate legislation. Administrative directions whose force is borrowed from CPA §§ 18 and 21. No independent penal machinery. Untested against an ultra vires challenge — far weaker than the EU GCD or Australian Consumer Law.
  • Puffery carve-out. "Obvious hyperboles" that do not deceive are permitted. "Committed to a sustainable future" / "on our journey to net-zero" can be recast as aspiration. The "clear, actionable plans" rule for aspirational claims has no minimum for targets, timelines, budgets or third-party verification.
  • Regulates the claim, not the organisation. No board verification, audit-committee oversight, internal claims-compliance or director certification. A company can publish first and assemble support later.

📣 ASCI Green Claims Code (15 Feb 2024)

  • Covers express or implied claims across the whole advertisement.
  • Absolute claims ("eco-friendly", "sustainable") need strong data or credible accreditation — cannot be rescued by disclaimers, QR codes or website links.
  • Comparative claims ("greener") must state the basis of comparison and be evidenced.
  • General claims should reflect the full life cycle unless limits are clearly stated.
  • No advantage from the absence of substances not usually in competing products, or from mere legal compliance.
  • Carbon offsets: if the offset will not materialise within two years, that fact must be disclosed prominently.
  • 26 March 2024 DoCA–ASCI joint press: ASCI findings may be forwarded to CCPA — discretionary, not automatic.

🔍 Why ASCI Is Only a Screen

  • Non-binding: can seek modification or withdrawal; cannot compel, fine, or order corrective advertising.
  • Reactive and sample-based: complaints or suo motu picks — not the full market.
  • Remedy ≪ commercial benefit: withdrawal is a manageable reputational cost, not a legal risk.
  • Early Indian "natural / chemical-free / eco" disputes (detergents, mosquito repellents, ACs, soaps) are treated in the thesis as literature illustrations of claim-types, not verified precedents — but they show the concern predates both 2024 codes.

Four-part architecture: (1) BRSR standardised disclosure; (2) BRSR Core value-chain and assurance; (3) ESG-labelled debt against "purpose-washing"; (4) ERPs + PFUTP as external validation and enforcement. Later relaxations have weakened safeguards built for that purpose.

📉 Four Design Weaknesses of BRSR

  • Single, financial materiality — not double materiality. Own environmental impact need not be fully reported unless it hits financial performance.
  • Uniform template across sectors lets high-impact industries emphasise easy metrics and omit sector-critical ones.
  • No single GHG methodology — comparability is weak even when numbers appear.
  • Phased Core assurance — most listed entities still self-certify. Only the top 150 face mandatory "adequate assurance" on Core.

⚖️ SEBI Enforcement Reality (FY 2024-25)

  • 55 cases of financial misstatement concluded; PFUTP against 155 entities; 239 adjudication orders; aggregate penalties ₹28.2 crore — across securities misconduct generally, not a dedicated greenwashing docket.
  • Gensol Engineering (interim order 15 Apr 2025): prima facie fraudulent diversion, weak internal controls, rerouting of ring-fenced borrowings — SEBI probing fund-use even where the enterprise was publicly associated with clean-mobility claims.
  • Amarjeet Singh, WTM, FICCI ESG Summit (Jan 2025): a company claimed "no significant environmental impact" while facing environmental litigation; an automobile company claimed recycled input use without corresponding plastic data. Official confirmation that ESG misrepresentation is a recognised problem, not speculation.
  • ERP regime gaps: no minimum competence in environmental science / GHG verification; methodology disclosed only in general form, without factor weightings. Divergent ratings of the same issuer can enable greenwashing rather than expose it.

🏢 Pillar 3 — Board & MCA Accountability (§ 5.2.3)

This pillar asks not what the public claim said, but who approved it. Section 166(2) is the central corporate-law provision. The 1956 Act had no equivalent; the 2013 wording followed the J.J. Irani Committee and arrived with § 135 and the 2011 NVGs. If Parliament meant only guidance, it would not have put the duty in the operative directors' section with a § 166(7) penalty. The low ceiling, however, shows it was not designed with greenwashing-scale fraud in mind.

How to Read "for the protection of environment" Text · Context · Purpose
Lens Doctrinal point
Textual "And" puts environment on the same plane as company, employees, shareholders and community. The noun is "the environment" generally, not the firm's own footprint. "Protection" is an active obligation. Noscitur a sociis with "community" + Ranjitsinh importing EPA § 2(a) covers climate, biodiversity and the truthfulness of environmental claims.
Contextual § 166(3) due care and independent judgment bars rubber-stamping weak claims. §§ 166(4)–(5) bite where greenwashing yields undue commercial gain. §§ 134–135 (board report, CSR) sit adjacent; after the 2025 CER holding they form one scheme. §§ 241–244 are the NCLT doorway.
Comparative Textually stronger than UK CA 2006 § 172 (environment is only a factor directors must "have regard to" while promoting success for members). India has the broader duty and the weaker enforcement. The FCA can fine and courts can disqualify; India has the opposite pairing.
Tata Consultancy Services Ltd v. Cyrus Investments (2021) 9 SCC 449 ¶ 19.30
Section 166(2) creates three distinct fiduciary duties: (i) promote objects for members as a whole; (ii) act in the best interests of the company, employees, shareholders and community; (iii) protect the environment. Environment is a freestanding limb — not a sub-part of community welfare.
M.K. Ranjitsinh (Bustard case) — 2021 order + 2025 INSC 1472
April 2021: "environment" in § 166(2) takes the EPA § 2(a) meaning. Four propositions follow: independent limb; broad EPA meaning; cost is not a complete defence; CSR is environmental as well as philanthropic.
Justiciability — the standing vacuum

Three points support enforceability: § 166(7) attaches a penalty; Ranjitsinh treated the duty as operative; §§ 241–244 can reach the NCLT. The obstacle is standing. The Act names no class that can enforce the environmental limb directly. Shareholders may proceed derivatively — the remedy belongs to the company. Communities and NGOs have no explicit standing. The NGT cannot fill the gap: the Companies Act is not in Schedule I of the NGT Act. Rajeev Saumitra v. Neetu Singh (Delhi HC, 2016) confirms § 166 states duties to the company, not rights of shareholders. A genuine legislative gap — not a deliberate choice to make the duty unenforceable.

🏛️ MCA Has No ESG Vertical

  • Administers the Act through the ROC network and SFIO. Enforcement focus remains conventional corporate fraud, not ESG-related governance failure.
  • Proving a § 166(2) greenwashing breach needs LCA, carbon-accounting and impact-review capacity MCA does not have in-house.
  • LODR already requires independent directors, audit and stakeholder committees; BRSR requires board-level sign-off in theory. The 2024 Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance asked for mandatory board ESG committees with environmentally expert independent directors.

  • Recommended: dedicated ESG oversight body inside MCA; Companies Act amendment putting ESG into fiduciary duties with turnover-based penalties and director disqualification; sector-specific guidelines; MSME support; ESG chapters in MCA annual reporting.

Closing crux of § 5.2

India does not lack hooks. It lacks a system that makes the three pillars fire together. Consumer law regulates the published claim; securities law regulates the listed disclosure and the labelled rupee; company law names an environmental duty without an empowered enforcer. Against EU turnover penalties, the UK anti-greenwashing rule and ASIC's docket, a ₹5 lakh director ceiling and a near-empty enforcement record look distinctly permissive.

Chapter 5 Section 5.3 Sectoral Regulators Environmental Law

Broader Environmental Regulation and Sectoral Standards

Thesis § 5.3: five instruments outside the three pillars — EPA 1986 and its delegated rules, the NGT, FSSAI food claims, BIS Ecomark, and the Trade Marks Act — that supply baselines, remedies and labels, but still cannot police environmental misrepresentation as such.

Opening crux of § 5.3

These statutes are not anti-greenwashing codes. They set the legal floor against which a "green" claim can be tested (EPA), the only forum that can impose restoration-scale money (NGT), the only sectoral lexicon that actually defines "organic" and "natural" (FSSAI), a third-party mark that can justify a claim (Ecomark), and a gate on deceptive branding (Trade Marks Act). The greenwashing gap is that none of them is designed to try the deception itself unless it is tied to listed environmental harm or a food/trade-mark hook.

🌿 § 5.3.1 — Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and Delegated Rules

The EPA extends across India. Its object is to protect and improve the environment and prevent hazards to human beings, other living creatures, plants and property. For this chapter its importance is not advertising law — it is that it sets the objective legal standards against which environmental claims can be tested.

  • Central Government toolkit: environmental quality standards; pollutant discharge and emission limits; nationwide pollution-control plans; restrict or prohibit industries in specified areas; regulate hazardous substances; inspect premises; issue directions for prevention and control.
  • Core prohibition: no industry, operation or process may discharge or emit pollutants above prescribed standards.
  • Delegated detail: Schedule I (industry-specific discharge and emission standards); Schedule III (ambient air quality, especially noise); Schedule IV (motor-vehicle emissions); Schedule VI (general discharge standards).
Anti-greenwashing significance of the EPA

The EPA is not a direct anti-greenwashing statute. It supplies the baseline. If a company markets itself as "green" or "eco-friendly" while violating prescribed pollution standards, the claim is immediately weakened by its own legal non-compliance. The CCPA 2024 Guidelines operate alongside, not in place of, these standards.

⚖️ § 5.3.2 — The National Green Tribunal: Remedial Powers and Jurisdictional Limits

Section 20 and jurisdiction were mapped earlier. Here the thesis treats the NGT as an enforcement pathway that does not fit neatly inside the three pillars. Unlike consumer commissions, ASCI or even SEBI, it can order compensation, environmental restoration, monetary penalties tied to the scale of harm, expert committees, follow-up compliance supervision, and directions for further proceedings where its orders are ignored. That lets it respond in proportion to damage rather than being trapped by low fixed statutory ceilings.

In re LG Polymers India Pvt Ltd, O.A. No. 73/2020 (PHC) (NGT, May 2020)
After the Visakhapatnam styrene gas leak the Tribunal took suo motu cognisance and directed a deposit of ₹50 crore as interim compensation. The company was held strictly and absolutely liable. The case shows readiness to impose significant interim liability and to push accountability beyond the corporate entity alone — before a full quantification of harm.
Volkswagen India Pvt. Ltd., O.A. No. 345/2019 (NGT)
The Tribunal accepted evidence that diesel defeat devices had manipulated emission tests so vehicles appeared compliant while emitting far more in actual use. It imposed a ₹500 crore environmental-restoration penalty, far above the earlier interim deposit. It rejected the defence that absence of quantified damage should prevent liability, and held that the intent to deceive regulators is itself harmful to the environmental-governance framework.
Three Volkswagen Propositions That Travel to Greenwashing Thesis ratio
# Holding in Volkswagen India Why it matters for greenwashing
1 Environmental law can protect the integrity of environmental reporting and claims, not only respond to physical harm. A false "compliant / clean diesel" representation is itself the wrong — closest Indian judicial logic to greenwashing.
2 Penalties can be set with deterrence in mind, not tied narrowly to quantified loss. ₹500 crore restoration sits in a different league from CCPA ₹10–50 lakh or Companies Act ₹1–5 lakh.
3 Systematic deception about environmental performance is actionable misconduct. The logic readily extends to advertising and disclosure greenwashing — if a forum can hear it.
The greenwashing gap — NGT cannot become a general forum

Original jurisdiction under Section 14 is limited to substantial environmental questions arising under statutes listed in Schedule I. That Schedule does not include the Consumer Protection Act, the Companies Act, or the Food Safety and Standards Act. The Tribunal therefore cannot decide misleading environmental advertising, breach of directors' environmental duties, or false food labelling. An NGO may approach the NGT about unlawful emissions, but not about the same company's "carbon neutral" advertising. It can address the harm that greenwashing conceals, but usually not the deception itself. A further practical limit: important orders, including aspects of the Volkswagen penalty, may be challenged before the Supreme Court under Section 22, so pending appeals leave the Tribunal's reasoning uncertain. The NGT still matters — proportionate penalties, deception-as-harm reasoning, and suo motu powers — but it cannot by itself supply a full anti-greenwashing regime.

🥗 § 5.3.3 — FSSAI: Sectoral Regulation of Environmental Claims in Food

The food and beverage sector is one of the clearest sites of greenwashing in India. Claims such as "100% natural", "organic", "eco-friendly packaging" and "sustainably sourced" appear frequently on labels and in advertising and they directly affect consumer choices. The Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 consolidates food law, establishes FSSAI as the science-based apex regulator, and applies across India to all Food Business Operators.

🚫 The "100%" Claims Crackdown

  • Recent FSSAI advisories warn Food Business Operators against "100% pure", "100% natural" and "100% organic".
  • "100%" is not defined in the statutory framework. Such claims create an impression of absolute purity or superiority that is usually difficult to substantiate scientifically.
  • This is a direct attack on one of the most common forms of claim inflation.

🔨 Enforcement Channels

  • Section 53 FSS Act: penalties up to ₹10 lakh for misleading advertisements — liability extends to publishers and endorsers.
  • Licence suspension or cancellation for repeat offenders; prosecution under Sections 52 and 53.
  • Digital complaint channels: Food Safety Connect app and FoSCoS portal, including a consumer-facing "Report Misleading Claims" route — complaint-based surveillance at ground level.

🏷️ § 5.3.4 — BIS Ecomark Certification Scheme

A voluntary product-labelling mechanism meant to identify genuinely environment-friendly products and give consumers and regulators a benchmark above bare legal compliance. Administered by BIS under the BIS Act, 1986; launched in 1991 as the Eco Mark / Eco Label.

  • A product qualifies only if production, use or disposal reduces environmental harm over its life cycle compared with similar products.
  • Assessment factors: pollution potential; recyclable or biodegradable content; resource use; waste generation; energy use; disposal impacts; overall life-cycle performance.
  • Logo is an earthen pot — renewable resources, low energy use, absence of hazardous waste. Dual Eco Mark + ISI mark signals environmental criteria and applicable quality standards.
  • Process: apply through BIS product-certification channels; BIS inspects and tests samples through its laboratories and recognised labs.
  • Anti-greenwashing role: standardised, third-party verified basis for claims. Where the CCPA Guidelines prohibit vague and unsubstantiated claims, Ecomark supplies a clearer benchmark that can help justify them.

™️ § 5.3.5 — Intellectual Property and Labelling

Combines consumer protection and intellectual property to address deceptive environmental branding. The Trade Marks Act, 1999 is an indirect but useful tool because trademarks often shape the consumer's first impression.

  • Section 9(1)(b): absolute refusal where marks are deceptive or lack distinctiveness.
  • Section 9(2)(a): a mark cannot be registered if it is likely to deceive the public or cause confusion. A mark implying eco-friendly or carbon-neutral status without real substantiation may be refused.
  • Mere descriptiveness: Section 9 also blocks marks that consist only of indications of kind, quality or characteristics. Generic green terms such as "EcoClean" or "100% Green" may be refused if merely descriptive or misleading.
  • Passing off: available for unregistered marks and deceptive use of registered marks. Misusing a recognised seal such as the BIS Eco Mark or "certified organic" without certification can amount to false advertising and passing off. Remedies: injunctions, damages or account of profits, and destruction or erasure of infringing material.
  • Endorser liability: CCPA Guidelines bind manufacturers, service providers, traders, agencies and endorsers. The mark owner must ensure permitted use does not mislead. CCPA may bar endorsers for one to three years.
Closing crux of § 5.3

Taken together, the peripherals prove the same diagnosis as the three pillars. India already has measurable pollution standards, a tribunal that will treat regulator-deception as harm, a food regulator that has started to police absolute claims, a life-cycle eco-label, and a trademark filter on green branding. What it still lacks is a forum and a statute that treat environmental misrepresentation as the primary wrong — which is why the NGT, FSSAI, BIS and the Trade Marks Registry remain useful satellites, not a substitute architecture. That sets up the five systemic gaps in § 5.4.

Chapter 6 Section 6.1 Key Findings Thesis Summary

Key Findings of the Doctoral Study

The five numbered findings of Chapter 6 — the actual conclusions of the doctoral study — not a condensed paraphrase.

1 Greenwashing in India is no longer limited to misleading advertisements

It now appears across product labels, websites, sustainability reports, BRSR filings, ESG ratings, green debt and carbon-reduction claims. CPA 2019 and the CCPA Guidelines are necessary but structurally downstream: reactive, complaint-driven, message- after-publication, with a puffery carve-out and no board-level verification duty. Official data in § 2.5 show a recurring governance and market-integrity problem, not a fringe advertising irregularity.

2 CSR → ESG did not solve the truth and verification problem

Section 135 regulates spend, not core operations. BRSR increased volume and status of reporting, but assurance is phased in only for Core indicators, large unlisted companies sit outside, companies choose their own assurers, and later relaxations delayed value-chain disclosure. Greenwashing migrated into documents that look more official. Disclosure without verification, integrated oversight and deterrent consequences does not close the truth gap.

3 Strong legal basis, almost no enforcement

Section 166(2) is an express environmental fiduciary duty, strengthened by Tata-Mistry and M.K. Ranjitsinh (2024/2025 CER holding). Yet Chapter 3 found no reported Indian decision applying § 166(2) to environmental failure. Standing is narrow; § 166(7) is ₹1–5 lakh; commentary calls the limb a potential "dead letter". Indian law does not lack accountability principles — it lacks enforcement design.

4 Near-zero enforcement is a design failure

Stronger jurisdictions treat greenwashing as an upstream governance problem (OECD 2025). EU: CSRD double materiality + GCD ex-ante verification + CPC airline sweep. UK: FCA rule + CMA 10% turnover fines, increasingly aligned. Australia: 47 ASIC interventions in 15 months; AUD 11.3 million Mercer penalty. India has the components but not the connecting design — and has backslid on BRSR assurance.

5 Fragmentation and poor coordination

CCPA, SEBI, ASCI and sectoral regulators do not share one definition of "carbon neutral" / "eco-friendly" / "net zero". The same claim travels across packaging, ads, annual reports, BRSR and green-debt documents with no lead regulator, mandatory cross-referral, shared evidence protocol or unified database. Overlap without coordination produces forum-shopping and orphan cases.

Chapter 6 Section 6.2 Hypothesis Testing Empirical Validation

Testing the Research Hypotheses

The thesis was premised on three hypotheses (Chapter 6.2). Each is tested through the doctrinal and comparative study — and each is validated.

✅ Hypothesis 1 — VALIDATED

"Greenwashing in India is primarily a consequence of weak corporate governance and the limited operationalisation of board-level environmental responsibility."

  • Contemporary greenwashing travels through reports, ESG disclosures, net-zero narratives and futurewashing — so a consumer-only framing cannot address it comprehensively.
  • CPA 2019 / CCPA / ASCI allocate duties toward advertisements; they impose no duty on decision-makers and are reactive, complaint-driven, and weak on long-horizon claims.
  • The hypothesis does not claim MCA/SEBI lack power. It is supported because the corporate- governance angle is jurisprudentially underdeveloped — board oversight, internal controls and assurance choices are the upstream location of the problem.

✅ Hypothesis 2 — VALIDATED

"The existing legal framework does not impose sufficiently clear, stringent and deterrent liability for misleading environmental disclosures, thereby enabling corporations to avoid meaningful accountability."

  • Duties exist; the liability routes used in ordinary greenwashing cases are still too weak. § 166(2) is textually strong but standing is narrow; consumer law is ad-focused; BRSR assurance was later relaxed.
  • Detection is complaint-driven; verification raises the burden of proof; pillars almost never fire together. Hohfeld: a formal duty + a paper penalty ≠ real liability if triggers are uncertain.
  • Rival view (securities-fraud tools can be severe) only shows stronger tools exist at the margins — they are not a routine pathway. If expected legal cost stays below expected benefit, the law functions as a shield.

✅ Hypothesis 3 — VALIDATED

"In addressing the issue of greenwashing, the present legal mechanism involving different regulatory bodies is inchoate and incomplete."

  • Greenwashing is a lifecycle phenomenon. One claim can sit under packaging, advertising, annual reports, ESG filings and green-finance documents at once.
  • The system lacks harmonised definitions, lead-regulator rules, mandatory cross-referrals, joint investigation protocols and common verification standards.
  • Regulatory redundancy becomes strength only when coordinated. Overlap without coordination enables forum-shopping, regulatory arbitrage and "orphan" cases. The architecture is therefore inchoate (not fully developed) and incomplete (structurally gapped).

Amend § 177(9) Extend the vigil mechanism beyond internal directors/employees so environmental misrepresentation can be reported with victimisation safeguards External stakeholders currently have no protected channel into the board's control system
⚖️ Proposed proviso to Section 166(7) (verbatim thesis draft)
"Provided that where the contravention relates to the authorisation, approval, or failure to prevent the publication of a materially misleading environmental claim by or on behalf of the company, the director shall be liable to a penalty not exceeding 1% of the company's turnover in the preceding financial year, or ₹5 crore, whichever is higher and shall also be liable for disqualification under Section 164(2)(b) for a period not exceeding 3 years."
